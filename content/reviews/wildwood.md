Laika founder Phil Knight and his son Travis are probably the only film studio owners on Earth who are unbothered by the financial success of their films. Almost every one of Laika’s films has grossed less than the last, yet their latest release, *Wildwood*, is by far their most expensive. Given that Phil Knight, who also founded Nike, is one of the fifty richest men in the world, the losses the film is projected to make are but a drop in the bucket for him. What a luxury it is for the animation industry to have a company solely dedicated to making these passion projects, pushing the boundaries of stop-motion, clocking in at 140 minutes, *Wildwood* is said to be the longest stop-motion film in history! And you can see every penny of it laid bare on screen.

Based on the novel of the same name, *Wildwood* follows Prue McKeel (Peyton Elizabeth Lee), a teenager whose infant brother Mac gets abducted by a murder of crows to a forest, leading her and her classmate Curtis (Jacob Tremblay) to chase after them. Though the forest beside Portland is supposedly impenetrable to outsiders, the vines make way for Prue’s arrival, where the dynamic duo discover a world of talking animals, spells, legends, and bureaucratic city hall employees. Will Prue manage to bring her baby brother back home?

The animation here is expectedly stunning. One completely forgets that the characters are puppets because their movements and expressions are seamlessly rendered. The Impassible Wilderness is a feat in production design, feeling magical and full of wonder while also managing to convey the dark and evil history that lurks underneath. Over a hundred handcrafted sets that would be more than worthy of an Oscar nomination for production design, a feat no animated film has yet been able to achieve.

Which makes it all the more frustrating that the screenplay here is just… fine. Everything novel about it was, I imagine, taken straight from the source material: the plot, premise, universe, and general structure. Where it struggles is in the smaller moments. Expositional dialogue, baffling character decisions, overlong flashback sequences, and maybe worst of all, weak characterisation. Let’s just say no girl is going to want to dress up as Prue next Halloween; an incredibly uninteresting character through and through. Nothing about her is memorable; she has no discernible traits that make her feel like a three-dimensional character that I want to root for. In a world full of such fascinating personalities, somehow the blandest character of them all is the protagonist.

The flaws in the script are such that one would easily mistake this for a family-friendly film for all audiences. The dialogue is simplistic, and there’s a general immaturity to the way these characters interact. Which I have no gripes with; it’s what should be expected for a film aimed at younger audiences. Yet because of the Knights’ indifference to profitability, they allowed themselves to include some severe violence and profanity that felt jarring. *Wildwood* is rated PG-13, yet much of the dialogue was more dumbed down and childish than that of the G-rated *Toy Story* films. I struggle, then, to understand exactly whom this film is meant for.

Qualms aside, this is still a mostly enjoyable watch. What does work works well enough to engage its audience for the hefty runtime (which breezed by quicker than I expected it to), while the incredible, textured animation makes it more than worth your while. Given its glowing reviews, it’s an absolute lock for the Animated Feature nomination, and I can’t see any contender strong enough to overtake it. *Coraline* may have been unlucky to be up against the timeless classics that are *Up* and *Fantastic Mr. Fox*, but seventeen years after their debut feature, Laika may finally get the industry recognition it deserves.
