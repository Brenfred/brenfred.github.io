Twelve years after *Top Five* saw Chris Rock exploring what it’s like to be a black man with an alcohol addiction in show business, his follow-up *Misty Green* sees the director wander into brand-new territory: exploring what it’s like to be a black woman with a cocaine addiction in show business. Nothing wrong with a filmmaker having a niche, of course, but I would’ve hoped a gap that large between films with such thematic similarities would’ve resulted in something more polished.

*Misty Green* follows the title character, played by Rosalind Eleazar, a has-been TV actress trying to land a role before she gets kicked out of her apartment. Her search brings her back into contact with Jordan (Chris Rock), a director she let down back in her heyday who is now helming a remake of *Dreamgirls*. Filling out the cast is an ensemble of A-listers, including Anna Kendrick, Daniel Kaluuya (in his first screen appearance since *Nope*), Adam Driver, and Topher Grace.

Unlike *Top Five*, which relies heavily on Rock’s vast experience in stand-up comedy, with its array of humorous anecdotes and vignettes, *Misty Green* has the bones of a tragic melodrama, requiring Rock to flex his storytelling muscles. Unfortunately, he’s largely unsuccessful at this endeavour, with the film failing to sidestep the clichés of its genre, while missing the charm that made *Top Five* so memorable.

Some of Rock’s creative decisions here are so baffling that I was wondering if they were intentionally so. There’s a scene in which Misty and Jordan are talking in a bar, and it cuts forward to different topics of conversation in multiple instances, yet it lacks any visual or audio clues to make these jumps clear to the audience. Only after a few seconds of listening to the dialogue does one realise they’re talking about something new, and that we’ve cut forward in time. Then there’s another scene where Misty is delivering a powerful monologue at an audition, and we see the video camera frame lines and the big “REC” in the top corner as if we were watching the audition tape footage, yet the camera inexplicably begins to dolly forward until we get a close-up of Misty, despite it supposedly being static on a tripod. Once again, I thought to myself, “Surely this isn’t a mistake; surely there’s some creative reason the filmmaker opted to do this.” The one scene that really shows us how talented Misty really is, one of the few genuinely emotional scenes of the film, and yet we leave it slightly confused. Why would Rock have wanted to do that? Both of these examples are too blatant to not be distracting, but too subtle to be an explicit creative decision.

The ensemble thankfully elevates the material. Eleazar is completely convincing in her role. Driver’s character is a somewhat clichéd antagonist, but he manages to sell it. Kaluuya steals every scene he’s in; his depiction of schizophrenia is so harrowing that you’d expect him to be in awards conversation if it had been in a stronger film. Kendrick’s character is obsessed with black men, and yeah, I bought it. Once again, though, Rock proves to be the master of his own demise, as the clear weak link in the cast. His scenes with Eleazar are some of the most compelling on a writing level — his character makes an observation that feels taken straight out of one of Rock’s stand-up routines — but his pouty expressions fail to deliver on an emotional level.

I respect *Misty Green* for what it aims to achieve. A critique on racism and sexism in the film industry. In that regard, the film does often succeed. But when it doesn’t, it’s because the storytelling undermines the message. Misty’s biggest struggles throughout the film aren’t due to her being a victim of these societal ills, but because she’s a self-destructive character. Kaluuya’s character frequently claims to be racially profiled, and he is, but his schizophrenia threatens to make his outbursts feel like paranoia and a symptom of his condition. Kendrick’s character fetishises black men, but this is at no point depicted as an issue. I applaud the film for exploring these topics, and being biting in its critique, but these contradictions often weaken the messaging.

As far as awards are concerned, there’s a chance Eleazar picks up a critics prize here or there, or potentially even breaks into Best Comedy Actress at the Globes (although there isn’t much that’s funny about her character). In my [breakdown of the Best Actress category](/review.html?slug=best-actress-breakdown) for the Oscars, I had preemptively predicted Eleazar in my fifth slot, but I will have to rescind that prediction having seen the film. Not that lone Actress nominees are particularly rare, but those are usually reserved for more established names like Ana de Armas in *Blonde*, or Kate Hudson in *Song Sung Blue*.

This was my first-ever TIFF screening, and what an apt way to kick things off. A film about the film industry, premiering at the festival, finally facing the opinions of the masses and critics (and ultimately coming up short, based on the other reactions I’ve seen). It’s the kind of film I’d have seen the reviews for and skipped altogether, had I not attended the festival, but I’m ultimately glad I did, if just to feel the collective awe of the crowd upon discovering at the Q&A that the lead actress is, in fact, British.
