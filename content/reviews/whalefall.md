*Whalefall* is a tense, operatic survival thriller with a ridiculous premise: what if you were swallowed by a whale and had to find a way to survive? On top of that, our erstwhile protagonist Jay is dealing with an intense case of daddy issues. Despite this, the film's old-school charm, freewheeling flashbacks, and simplicity are just earnest enough to win over the open-hearted viewer.

The selling point of this movie is a countdown: Jay is stuck in a sperm whale's stomach with an air tank, giving him a limited amount of time to plan his escape. In the tradition of films like *Buried* and *127 Hours*, it's an irresistible premise. What would you do if you found yourself in this situation? How will our hero survive?

The circumstances leading up to the swallowing are laid out clearly, and his inventory of tools is set up as if in a video game. At every step, we know exactly what he has to work with, and rejoice in his new discoveries alongside him. In fact, these scenes can get so claustrophobic and tense that when flashbacks start arriving they provide a welcome respite. More on that later!

Outside of our captive's chamber, a vast, beautiful environment unfolds. The underwater photography here is remarkable, at times faintly recalling the splendor of *Avatar: The Way of Water*, albeit in a more grounded, budget-conscious manner. The sequence in which our hero gets trapped, involving a voracious giant squid, is thrilling and makes the most of this film's combination of CGI and practical effects. At one point Jay finds himself on the precipice of a vast underwater chasm, and the use of light and darkness allows our imagination to fill in the gaps.

Not unlike outer space, the ocean is a beautiful, terrifying, fundamentally unknowable expanse. I have a soft spot for space exploration movies that manage to convey this because it is unlike anything I will ever experience, and although this movie only touches the surface of exploring this, I was impressed with the feelings it managed to evoke with limited time and resources. One sequence involving a large gathering of underwater creatures in the blackest depths of the sea felt particularly magical, using its obvious CGI coupled with swelling, romantic score to evoke an alien experience.

However, the emotional core of this film is not found underwater: it is in Jay's conflicted feelings about his deceased father. The first few minutes establish this dynamic: his father (Josh Brolin, giving a blunt-force evocation of traditional masculinity in broad strokes), having always loved diving, chooses to go out on his own terms when faced with a terminal illness and takes one last dive, his body never to be recovered. Jay is introduced to us as a broken shell of a young man, holding his unresolved feelings inside despite his mother and sisters' attempts to care for him and get him to open up. Flashbacks throughout the film slowly reveal how he came to be this way, as we see his turbulent relationship with his father in stark detail. The editing choices perfectly cut the tension, cutting to an intense memory with his father every time Jay's plight within the whale threatens to get unbearably tense and claustrophobic. The dynamics of their relationship tread familiar territory regarding "tough love" and the high expectations fathers can place on their sons, but the film avoids feeling too rote by operating in an operatic, grand manner. These memories are colored by the intensity of the feelings Jay holds inside and exacerbated by his quickly dwindling air supply.

This approach culminates in a fantastical, bordering on silly finale, literalizing the tie between Jay's daddy issues and his underwater plight. The film walks a very tight rope to get to this point with aplomb, making it work on the backs of Austin Abrams' strong performance and the film's freewheeling sense of style. In a time when our culture is obsessed with art and entertainment feeling as "real" as possible, writer-director Brian Duffield dares to harken back to a time in which audiences were more willing to be transported to fantastical spaces that operate on movie logic, guided by strong movie star performances.

Speaking of stars, Austin Abrams is one of our best rising ones in the current moment. Coming off of his scene-stealing role in last year's *Weapons* as a lovably dumb junkie, and immediately following this fall's star turn in the smash hit franchise reboot *Resident Evil* (in which he is similarly tasked with carrying the audience through the entire film), here Abrams displays a less funny and charming side of himself. His character here is deeply depressed and in denial about his feelings, which would be hard enough to play without also being tasked with making us feel what it would be like to be trapped inside a living creature with minutes to live. At times he displays his immaturity as an actor, resorting to repeating the same emotions of distress, but overall he handles this with aplomb. His casting shows shrewdness on Duffield's part, as his natural charm is weaponized to prevent this character from being too dour and unlikeable as he continually rejects everyone's attempts to help him express his feelings. At times the film feels like it could use some of Abrams' trademark humor to lighten the intensity, but that's a minor quibble when the fast-paced screenplay and freewheeling editing keeps this thing gliding along so smoothly.

Unfortunately, the majority of audiences have not been on *Whalefall*'s wavelength thus far, as it released last weekend to underwhelming box office and mildly positive reviews. It seems the old school approach is not what most people want right now, but if you are open to it, I would encourage you to give this film a chance. If awards bodies choose to ignore its underperformance, there are several craft elements here that would be well deserving: visual effects, sound, original score, and makeup.

*Whalefall* is a film of simple pleasures: survival thrills, exploration of the natural world, a star turn from current it-boy Austin Abrams. You may not leave with a greater understanding of father-son dynamics, but you will gain an appreciation for the undersea world and feel your heart swell with the inspiring story of a broken young man facing his demons head on. Come for the whale, stay for Abrams' charming performance and Duffield's old-school techniques!
