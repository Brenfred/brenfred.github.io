I first watched “It Ends” during its initial limited release on the Letterboxd Video Store in December 2025, where the groundswell of online support led indie distributor Neon to take a risk and pick up the film. Since then, I have seen the film twice more, which has only made the dialogue and dynamics between the four characters stand out more, even as the film’s limitations became more noticeable. Made on a shoestring “micro-budget” of $150,000, “It Ends” is my favorite directorial debut of the year so far, largely because director Alexander Ullom understands that the people within the car are vastly more interesting than any cliche horror film entity lurking in the woods.

For the entirety of the film’s compact 89-minute runtime, the film follows four friends — James (Phinehas Yoon), Day (Akira Jackson), Fisher (Noah Toth) and Tyler (Mitchell Cole) — who embark on a late-night drive for food before turning onto a forest drive that becomes an endless road. Beneath a supernatural-thriller surface, Ullom crafts an innately “Gen Z” story about four college graduates struggling with the uncertainty of post-graduate life. After turning onto this never-ending road, the friends quickly discover the entrance has closed behind them and that stopping the car for too long attracts hordes of crazed strangers from the woods. Other contrived rules reveal themselves as the story progresses, including that the gas tank never empties, the characters never feel hungry or tired, and injuries magically heal.

Throughout the first act, “It Ends” operates as a conventional horror film. Once James chooses to leave the car, however, the film shifts to a notably lighter tone, letting the horror fade into the background in favor of a hangout comedy focused on conversation and camaraderie. From this perspective, the film often feels like a hidden Richard Linklater movie rather than a traditional horror movie. Much like Linklater’s classics, such as “Dazed and Confused” and “Everybody Wants Some!!,” the film’s greatest successes are when it perfectly captures conversations between young people who are stuck at a crossroads in their lives. Jokes like Day complaining she was about to download the classic mobile game “Subway Surfers” right before they turned onto the road, while she and Fisher create memes about the road itself, work so naturally.

However, these scenes work only because the performances are just as natural as the script itself. In an August 20 Q&A I attended at AMC The Grove 14 in Los Angeles, Ullom explained he posted open casting calls on social media to find actors who could work off the dialogue and expand on what was on the page. Yoon, Jackson and Toth, in particular, speak with the ease of people who have known each other for years, making the viewer feel like another friend in the car. Cole’s performance as Tyler is noticeably awkward, but that could be a deliberate choice, since he was away from the group for the last year. However, one choice regarding Tyler that cannot be explained away so easily is how early he is written out of the film. After driving thousands of miles, Tyler chooses to exit the car and stay behind in the forest, leaving the other three characters to continue on. This decision makes sense within the story and sets the psychological stakes, but Ullom doesn’t give the audience much time to care for Tyler before cutting him out. Truthfully, the dynamic of the gang only becomes more interesting after his departure.

Tyler’s exit still establishes the film’s central idea — that their lives after graduation have no obvious next destination. James’ insistence that he has to work a “boring” job with high pay and Day’s plans to move back in with her family while she gets on the “ZipRecruiter grind” contribute to this theme. Ullom’s road is less a horror tactic and more a literalization of this post-grad uncertainty. The characters can drive forward forever on the road without knowing its destination — or even if there is a destination at all.

Even with this strong theme, “It Ends” lacks technical consistency with its minuscule budget. The entire movie takes place in or just outside of the same car and, by the third watch, the repetition definitely begins to take a toll. Some driving scenes around the middle also reveal limitations, especially when the trees outside the windows seem to stutter unnaturally.

These issues, however, do little to take away from what Ullom does best here. No matter where the box office gross ends up, “It Ends” has clearly established Ullom as a young filmmaker to watch. Neon is backing his next feature, “4 X 4: The Event,” which should be considerably larger and includes Sophia Lillis, Dylan Minnette, Jacob Tremblay, Jaden Smith and returning “It Ends” star Cole. It’s easy to see why the studio would want a bet on Ullom’s future.
