Year after year, the Sundance Film Festival provides blossoming filmmakers a platform to reach the world with all sorts of films. The festival often features an assortment of horror films, where many find their way to a major audience, such as *I Saw the TV Glow* and *Talk to Me*. In this year’s edition of the prestigious festival, the most buzzed-about film within the genre was *Leviticus*.

Following its debut at Sundance and subsequent acquisition by NEON, it would go on to be a highly respected and acclaimed horror film, with notable appearances at other film festivals such as South by Southwest. Another festival it played at was the Miami Film Festival, where I saw the film prior to its recent wide release.

*Leviticus* is the feature debut of queer director Adrian Chiarella. The film follows Naim, played by Joe Bird (*Talk to Me*), and the malevolent circumstances that have caused him to be hunted by a creature who takes the look of the one he loves most. Chiarella has stated that his sense of dread over whether his family would accept his homosexuality while growing up was a large inspiration for him working on this film. One viewing of the movie makes it clear how intensely he channeled his experience into the film. In the end, while I deeply appreciate the film’s first act and some of its incredibly strong filmmaking, the central conceit of the film is the unfortunate catalyst for its clumsy utilization of the horror genre.

*Leviticus* is tonally split into two parts. The first is a queer religious thriller, and it is where the film shines. Chiarella’s skill in creating mystery and dread is on full display as he constructs every scene, instilling a sense of discomfort. Practically every scene in both parts of *Leviticus* utilizes this, but the first part takes it beyond physical discomfort, with a nuanced sense of despair washing over the environment. If a scene isn’t crushing you with horrific sound design, you feel a quiet sense of hatred emanating from every character. Even the scene that brings us into the second part of the film instills a horrifying feeling, as if the horrific scene is inevitable.

I could sing a lot of praises about the first part or so of the film, but a lot of the film’s strengths appear within both halves, so before speaking on the second, I’d like to highlight one of the movie’s strongest attributes: its ensemble of characters. Evidently leading the cast is the central relationship between Naim and Ryan, played by Stacy Clausen. I really like their chemistry. The connection between the two is executed in such a way that it feels wrong to spoil, but it’s unique and interesting to watch play out.

Despite the effective performances of the lead duo, they do not stand out as best in show. That honor goes to Australian actress and one of the film’s producers, Mia Wasikowska, who plays the mother of Naim. In an interview with James A. Janisse of Dead Meat, Chiarella spoke about working with the actress and how he approached her for participation in the film, and I could not be more thankful that he did.

Wasikowska’s performance is eerily subtle, serving as one of the film’s biggest jumping-off points for its commentary. Her character leans on the film’s portrayal of disguising homophobia as worry and love, and her performance is fantastic at portraying this. While she may stand out, the film features a cast with great performances, such as those of Nicolas Hope, Ewen Leslie, and Shannon Berry. Although some of them feature within the second half, the shift is when most of the supporting cast begins to dwindle. As the main characters begin to become isolated, the flaws plaguing the film come to light.

The horror genre is one of the most underrated genres because so much of it involves repetitive uses of the same old tropes. *Leviticus*’s biggest issue is how much it falls into this same trap within its second half. While the film’s horror sounds extremely reminiscent of *It Follows*, the full concept promises to provide a unique evolution. Instead, it just rehashes so much of what staples the horror genre and modern filmmaking: dumb character decisions for the sake of moving the plot, the use of the same common tropes (third-act breakup, liar revealed, etc.), random comedic breaks in tension that provide zero impact, and massively underdeveloped characters.

The last one is especially troubling when the film’s 88-minute runtime leaves it stuck with exclusively Naim’s perspective, a decision that makes all but 2-3 characters one-dimensional and flat, including the character of Ryan. The decision also causes a complete lack of mystery as to whether Naim is facing the monster or Ryan, but the movie insists there is a mystery within most scenes, which keeps making it feel clunky. The script itself really takes a nosedive as soon as the film switches from thriller to true horror.

It’s a true shame that the second half struggles so badly to find its footing when it’s far from devoid of merit. The film itself is fantastic on a technical level all the way through. I can speak of the makeup, the editing, and much more, but within these, the standouts are the sound design and cinematography. The sound is usually quiet and meditative, a commonality of the film’s best moments, but it does also work to make the horror feel tense. Every moment it surges, it feels appropriately brutal, making it one of the film’s last remaining tools to create tension by the film’s end. The other tool left is the film’s cinematography. It’s a stunning-looking film, with beautiful skyline shots mixed with expert blocking to make the suspenseful scenes work.

I would be remiss if I talked about a film with as much social commentary as *Leviticus* and completely ignored everything it has to say. The first half is evidently where we get the most. A large chunk of it is best described as a scathing condemnation of a toxic religious culture, which doesn’t just bring unnecessary hate toward the queer community, but frames it as hate itself.

Within the second half, we get the monster and the central allegory, which is probably the film’s most contested aspect. I’ve seen many consider it to be representative of conversion therapy, and while I somewhat agree, I find it to be a wider representation of hate crimes as a whole. It’s an unfortunately inevitable entity, conjured of hatred. You can’t reason with it; its only purpose is to hurt you. It will pretend to be there to help and claim a moral high ground, when it really just stands to spread dissent and rage toward innocent people.

At the end of the day, I can’t say *Leviticus* is bad at all. The excellent first half is engaging and a perfect start. The ensemble manages to create engaging, if one-note, characters. It’s well done technically in most aspects. Even when the second half arrives and the film becomes a shell of its former self, it remains an engaging and meaningful watch. Unfortunately, it still does lead to the film’s decline, making for a noticeable split in quality. While *Leviticus* manages to rise above some of the worse horror movies in recent memory, I can’t say it’s at the level of the new horror debuts that have taken the spotlight.
