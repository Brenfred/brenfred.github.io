“You are a carcinogen...”

From the first few seconds of this film, the deep brass tones of this voice bring us into the hellscape of filmmaker David Lowery’s mind. This is not a fairy tale, or a sing-along musical experience. You will not get to mindlessly bop your head along to songs written by Jack Antonoff, Charli xcx, and FKA twigs for two hours, nor is this an opportunity to cheer for the mother-off of the year between Michaela Coel and Anne Hathaway. This is a painful, bruising exploration of the gnarly ideas we don’t like to think about, brought to light in exquisite and mesmerizing detail. This is a nightmare.

Lowery is a director that seemingly exists in the constant tension of the “one for you, one for me” mindset that Hollywood traps many an auteur within. Bouncing from meditative works like *A Ghost Story* and *The Green Knight* to Disney live-action noise like *Pete’s Dragon* and *Peter Pan & Wendy*, the only consistency with his work is that you’ll never quite know what he’s bringing to the table until you watch his newest film for yourself. This maelstrom of ideas is doubled in intensity for his newest outing, *Mother Mary*, a film about a pop star who’s definitely not Taylor Swift that seeks out her former costume designer and ex-best friend requesting a new outfit for her upcoming concert. As these two silently broken people reunite, they are forced to reckon with the fallout of their past connection, and the ways in which it has affected their present to a supernatural degree.

Fans of Lowery’s work will know that this movie was broiling in development hell for a few years now, with the esteemed filmmaker claiming year after year that he was lost in the editing bay, unable to discover what this movie truly had to say. When you watch the final product, you can tell, but in all the best ways. *Mother Mary* is brimming with so many ideas it’s an honest miracle that they all fit within a two hour narrative; perhaps more impressively, they weave into each other and around a central throughline that proves this to be one of the most urgently affecting cinematic experiences of the year thus far.

Michaela Coel and Anne Hathaway are two tours de force here, each setting out on a mission to extend their characters far beyond the written page and bringing us into instant discourse with them. Coel brings a voice thrumming with power, yet with an undercurrent of pain beneath her words, as she strides across the screen with a confidence that seems *too* poised, the strut of someone who doesn’t want the people around her to see how she’s suffered. Hathaway shows a more vulnerable side of herself to balance against this, with carefully trembling movement and a noted focus to her eyes that grant her a piercing intensity. There’s rarely a moment where one of these two aren’t on screen, and you’d never want such a moment to occur; what really makes this shine is seeing two awards-worthy actresses giving masterclass work side by side.

The technical marvels on display here shouldn’t be understated. Cinematographers Andrew Droz Palermo and Rina Yang create a visual language unlike any I’ve seen in a long while, where the frame takes on a life of its own. Bina Daigeler’s costume design is a sight to behold, with dozens upon dozens of the most gorgeous dresses you’ve ever seen quite literally taking your breath away as you see them pass across the silver screen. The music is a character within the film itself; while this is not a wall-to-wall musical, this is a film where the only way to truly understand Mary is through her music, and through the songs one can begin to access the deeper themes being discussed.

I will refrain from deeply analyzing what this film has to say to avoid ruining its surprises, but I will say that Lowery clearly has a fundamental human concept on his mind, and it feels distinct from what he’s had to say in the past. This movie wants you to seriously consider what your basic emotions actually say about you, and investigate the sordid shadows cast by the shining lights of life that we assume have no dark side. This is the other side of a euphoric coin, a film that finds horror where other work would find inspiration. This is a deeply unsettling, discomforting tale that won’t leave your mind for days after the credits roll, and the further you delve into it, the more you’ll discover about not just these characters, but about yourself.

*Mother Mary* is a striking, painfully significant work. It’s the type of movie that can only be born of unabashed, unfiltered creativity. Movies like this are standout examples of how far you can go with cinema as an artform. An unforgettable cinematic experience.
