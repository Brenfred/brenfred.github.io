Year after year, there’s a discussion about why we’re remaking an iconic piece of literature, an award-winning film, or even a relatable, age-old, decade-defining story…and usually, the answer to why is simple. It’s money. It’s introducing this story to a new generation. But what I personally like to say is the most important answer to this question: it’s allowing new voices to tell these stories and connect them to new generations.

That’s exactly what Georgia Oakley brings to the table with her adaptation of *Sense and Sensibility*.

Oakley is a BAFTA-nominated director for her 2022 film, *Blue Jean*, and now she has gotten her big studio break, directing Focus Features’ retelling of the Jane Austen classic. If you’ve read Austen’s 1811 book or seen Ang Lee’s coveted 1995 film, you know what you’re in for. What makes the 2026 adaptation stand apart, though, is Oakley’s eye behind the camera.

The visual identity of *Sense and Sensibility* is gorgeous. I can’t help but fall in love with the beautiful visuals she brings to the screen, whether it’s her use of natural light, her creativity with mirrors, or something that came and went throughout the film but, when it was presented, was incredibly effective… her camera movements. It has a purpose inside the scene, often moving around like it’s a character itself, making you feel like you’re another sister in the Dashwood family.

I just wish that same creativity was extended to the story at large.

Now, here is where I admit something: I am not a huge fan of period costume dramas. Typically, the ones that break the mold and work for me switch up the style and add something unique to the conversation. Think of Yorgos Lanthimos’ *The Favourite* and how it uses absurdist comedy to differentiate itself from the normal costume drama. Think of Ridley Scott’s *Napoleon*, which goes all out with its grand-scale spectacle. Or even Greta Gerwig’s *Little Women*, another iconic book-to-screen adaptation that makes you really fall in love with these characters all over again.

Even as someone who has never read *Sense and Sensibility*, I basically know the story, whether from previous interpretations or simply because the material is so iconic. It has transcended its original form so much that you probably know the beats without even realizing it.

Following the death of their father, *Sense and Sensibility* follows the Dashwood sisters, Elinor and Marianane, who due to the laws of the time are forced to leave their family estate when the house is left to the eldest (estranged) brother and have to start over alongside their mother and little sister in the quiet countryside. The sisters must adapt to a new lifestyle without the wealth, having to face societal expectations head on, and encounter complicated romantic endeavors while discovering their very different approaches to both life and love.

And Oakley’s retelling basically falls in line with the story you would assume from the outside. You’re never really left surprised by what plays out on screen. But what does grab your attention is Oakley’s distinct visual style, which cements her as a filmmaker on the rise and someone to keep an eye on in the future.

Beyond the beauty of *Sense and Sensibility*, the costumes and sets are fantastic showcases. They transport you back to this time, and when working in tandem with Arnaud Poitier’s cinematography, you are having a ball and dazzled by the spectacle. It’s the kind of movie where you can almost forget how you feel about the story because you're so absorbed in watching it.

Then there’s Marianne Dashwood, portrayed by Esme Creed-Miles. She really is a breath of fresh air in the story. In every scene she graces, she commands your attention and shows why she is one of the most buzzy up-and-coming actors. Her performance has an energy that feels completely different from everything happening around her.

That’s kind of odd to say when she’s starring alongside Daisy Edgar-Jones, someone whose name is essentially what this film is being billed around. But I really gravitated more toward Creed-Miles’ portrayal of Marianne, which is filled with life, both the highs and the lows, and found the story to be more focused on her and her journey.

While Edgar-Jones’ Elinor is the focus of *Sense and Sensibility*, in my opinion her relevance gets pushed to the back, and her scenes felt less impactful than those revolving around her sister. I would have loved to see more time of Elinor bonding with Edward Ferrars to make their inevitable relationship arc feel more impactful. We got to spend time with Marianne and see the joys and sorrows of her connections to Willoughby and Colonel Brandon play out in real time, while Elinor and Edward were reduced to one conversation and a montage.

I think that speaks to the film’s story at large. Creed-Miles commands your attention while she’s on screen to the point where it feels like her scene partners and the rest of the ensemble are sometimes in another movie. They are all so grounded, so plain, and, as discussed in the film, they lack that sense of passion. At times, it can make you a little bored.

Overall, while I may not fancy *Sense and Sensibility* the way some will, the visual identity and technical design of Oakley’s adaptation is absolutely my cup of tea. I think this film will work so well for its intended demographic, but that target audience sadly isn't me. And honestly, that’s okay! A movie doesn’t have to be made for me to appreciate what it’s doing well.

So don’t let my mixed reaction keep you hesitant from checking out Focus Features’ newest release, because it’s absolutely worth your time when it hits cinemas on October 16.
