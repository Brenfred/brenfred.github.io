Dinosaurs have captured the imaginations of children and adults alike for decades. The idea of gigantic, terrifying, majestic lizards roaming the earth is inherently fascinating, and the mystery of a mass extinction event only adds to the intrigue. In 1993, the popular concept was changed forever with the massive success of Steven Spielberg’s *Jurassic Park*. *The End of Oak Street* adds a fun, unique chapter to the legacy of the subgenre that sprung up in the wake of that film’s success.

Spielberg is a major influence on this movie in more ways than one. It starts off in a picturesque 1980s American suburb; our protagonists, the Platt family, follow a familiar structure for Amblin movies from that era. Denise (Anne Hathaway) is a frustrated mother working on her novels in secret. Greg (Ewan McGregor) hides a drinking problem and a second career as a pizza delivery driver from her because he doesn’t want to let her know that he’s been laid off. Audrey (Maisy Stella) is a science nerd living in a time when that is not a socially acceptable interest for a teenage girl to have. Brian (Christian Convery) is bullied at school and tries desperately to be seen as manly by his peers. Each character is sketched just enough to give us a clear idea of their interests, desires, and flaws before the action starts, and we get to watch how they react to extreme circumstances.

The premise of this movie is simple: a suburban neighborhood is suddenly and mysteriously transported to prehistoric times. The Platt family has one goal: team up and survive long enough to have a chance to return home. The film’s strength is in its simplicity: unlike in the recent *Jurassic Park* sequels, the dinosaurs here are simply the monsters that have to be survived. There is a science fiction element here, but it is not over-explained. Another thing that makes the film unique is the design of the creatures themselves. Raptors and giant predators are given feathers and birdlike movements, reflecting our evolving scientific understanding of what these creatures might have looked like.

Thrills, chills, and character-based drama: David Robert Mitchell delivers it all here. His filmography may look eclectic at first glance. *It Follows* is the iconic horror film in which the monster is transferred from person to person like an STD. *Under the Silver Lake* is a psychological thriller full of hidden symbols that reflect the protagonist’s own ultimately pointless journey. By comparison, this film is surprisingly straightforward, a tribute to Spielbergian simplicity and classic suburban values. The thing that ties these three films together, however, is a sense of wonder, mystery, and creeping dread. All three films contain mysteries that are ultimately never fully explained. They put their protagonists in difficult situations that gradually reveal themselves to be increasingly complex, unpredictable, and seemingly impossible to solve. There is a Lynchian sense of suburban bliss with a hidden dark undercurrent. Like John Carpenter, Mitchell uses music as an essential part of the atmosphere, allowing unexpected musical choices to heighten the tension. Although this film is shaping up to be a disappointment at the box office, we can only hope that Mitchell will continue to deliver on what promises to be a fascinating career.

## Sean’s Awards Corner!

Given its box office performance and general audience response, I would not expect this movie to do much on the awards circuit. The visual effects work is strong, although the lower budget than most dinosaur movies is apparent. I would expect this to show up in guild nominations for visual effects and sound, and it will likely appear on a couple of Oscar shortlists. Don’t expect to see any nominations at the Oscars or any major precursors, though.

*The End of Oak Street* is a fairly straightforward thrill ride that hearkens back to movies of old, from a simpler time, while also having characters complex enough to take the audience on a satisfying journey. Go see it in theaters while you still can!
