Jesse Eisenberg’s *The Debut* was undoubtedly my most anticipated film of 2026. His previous directorial effort, *A Real Pain*, was a remarkably witty and heartfelt comedy-drama whose sharp screenplay, subtle performances, and pitch-perfect direction made it one of my favorite films of 2024. The idea, therefore, of Eisenberg returning with an oddball comedy starring two of the industry’s most acclaimed actors, Julianne Moore and Paul Giamatti, was incredibly exciting.

*The Debut* premiered at the Telluride Film Festival in September to rave reviews and quickly emerged as a major Oscar contender, with early predictions even entertaining the possibility of a Best Picture nomination. After finally catching the film myself at the Philadelphia Film Festival, I can certainly understand where the enthusiasm comes from. However, I walked away somewhat unsatisfied, simply because Eisenberg’s new screenplay lacks the complexity of his previous work. While Moore and Giamatti deliver two uniquely exceptional performances in a frequently hilarious comedy, the second half struggles, leaving the film just short of true greatness.

Set in central New Jersey, *The Debut* follows stay-at-home mom Mona Friedman (Moore), who has clearly spent much of her adult life caring for a family that barely acknowledges her existence. With her two children preparing to graduate high school and a husband who takes little interest in her life, Mona yearns for a change. She auditions for a supporting role in a local community theater production of the fictional musical *Nosy Neighbors*, despite having no prior theater experience. Following a disastrous audition, Mona lands the part after another actress drops out and finds herself working under the intense direction of Jerry Blankenbuehler (Giamatti).

The role, that of a tough apartment-building superintendent named Miss Danielle, initially seems far too difficult for Mona. Her generally quiet personality leads to a less-than-inspiring performance, but Jerry begins pushing her to fully embrace the character. From there, the audience is treated to an increasingly absurd character transformation as Mona discovers the confidence she has been searching for her entire life, gradually blurring the line between Miss Danielle and herself. Moore is a true revelation as Mona, delivering perhaps my favorite leading performance of the year. She begins as painfully reserved before transforming into a truly cartoonish personality. This absurdity is ultimately the film’s greatest strength, turning what could have been a conventional story of self-discovery into something far more unpredictable and entertaining.

My favorite sequence comes during a rehearsal in which Jerry improvisationally challenges Mona to demand that he pay rent, pushing her to uncover the personal motivations behind her character. Moore delivers an extended, over-the-top monologue that is easily one of the film’s highlights. I'm certainly not alone in this assessment, as the sequence has inspired mid-screening applause at several festival screenings. My Philadelphia audience reacted similarly, breaking into raucous applause as Moore finished.

Giamatti is similarly excellent, matching Moore’s heightened comedic energy while providing a necessary counterweight to her increasingly bizarre behavior. His performance is admittedly familiar, recalling the gruff but ultimately caring personality he portrayed in the acclaimed *The Holdovers*. Still, Giamatti plays this character type exceptionally well, and his chemistry with Moore keeps their ridiculous interactions consistently entertaining. Together, the two actors elevate a script that I think would have been considerably less compelling without them.

My biggest issue with *The Debut* is the way the narrative flatlines after the aforementioned rehearsal sequence. Eisenberg spends the first half of the film gradually escalating both the comedy and drama, but the second half plateaus at the same level of absurdity rather than raising the stakes. I felt a noticeable loss of momentum here, as the payoff in the final act never quite reaches the excitement—or relief—of Mona and Jerry’s journey.

The screenplay also lacks much of the subtlety that made *A Real Pain* one of my favorite films. The message behind *The Debut* is admirable, using Mona’s character to explore more broadly how the arts can provide a sense of identity and community for someone who has spent years feeling invisible. The supporting characters, however, frequently veer toward caricatures rather than feeling fully fleshed out. Combined with an ending that is a little too neat, this distances the film from the nuance that made Eisenberg’s previous work so compelling.

*The Debut* looks to be a true awards juggernaut as we enter the fall season. Currently, I would predict nominations in Best Picture, Best Actress, Best Supporting Actor, Best Original Screenplay, Best Original Song, and perhaps even Best Casting. Moore is the current favorite to win Best Actress, with 84% of Award Expert users picking her as their first choice, while Giamatti and Eisenberg are also in contention for Best Supporting Actor and Best Original Screenplay, respectively. Given the film’s subject matter, which focuses heavily on acting, and the tremendous initial response to its leading performances, I suspect the Academy’s acting branch will be especially enthralled by *The Debut*.

*The Debut* is a hilarious ride featuring two of the year’s best performances. While my sky-high expectations were not fully met, I had a blast watching it, and even if it is unlikely to become one of my favorite films of the year, it remains a true celebration of finding oneself through performance.
