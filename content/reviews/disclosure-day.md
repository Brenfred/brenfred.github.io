The GOAT debate transcends medium. You could be talking hoops and arguing, "Is Michael Jordan or LeBron James a better player?" You could be talking about music and asking, "Is Michael Jackson or The Beatles the best of all time?" And when it comes to movies, a name that is often brought up is Steven Spielberg.

Lately, a lot of the GOAT-tier directors have been receiving a bit of backlash, whether that's Christopher Nolan and the online, stupid controversy regarding his choices for *The Odyssey*, it could be with Martin Scorsese and how certain pockets of the internet are not very receptive to his embrace of AI. For Steven Spielberg, I would say *Disclosure Day*, at least the first half of the movie, is a blemish on his record.

*Disclosure Day* stars Emily Blunt, a meteorologist, and a cybersecurity expert (Josh O'Connor), who find themselves at the center of a movement to expose the government's cover-up of extraterrestrial secrets. And that's just the tip of the iceberg. The screenplay, written by David Koepp, has too much going on and oftentimes just feels like it's jumping from one thing to the next without much substance, leaving the viewer behind. There are stretches in this movie where action is taking place on screen, but we're not learning anything new or, more importantly, grasping what we just saw.

But none of this matters when you have a performance like Emily Blunt's. Blunt is a true chameleon in *Disclosure Day*, delivering career-best work that I wish I could extend to the rest of this movie at large. The way that she nails this character and always keeps the audience guessing, whether she is changing language, dialect, body movement, or screen presence, Emily Blunt is the MVP of *Disclosure Day*. If this review does anything, it is to be the *Disclosure Day* of Emily Blunt supremacy. Between her work earlier this year in *The Devil Wears Prada 2*, where she absolutely steals the show, and her status as an often underrated performer, I'm glad that we finally have a specific performance to point to and say, "Hey, this is the best thing that you've done so far."

I am not an overly negative person, so I don't want to dive into how Josh O'Connor feels like a blank slate in the worst way possible, or that Eve Hewson is giving an absolute stinker of a performance. I also don't want to dive into all the ways this movie loses me throughout its runtime, with its convoluted plot, messy storytelling, or lack of any clear path forward. Instead, I want to play spin doctor and talk about the features of *Disclosure Day* that really resonated with me. I've already discussed Emily Blunt, but I was also absolutely enamored with the last 15 to 30 minutes of this movie. Once all of our plot lines finally converge at this Kansas City news station, I feel like Spielberg's vision comes to fruition. Using the production room of this news station as the catalyst for this film's message proved very effective. Now, is that partially because this is my line of work, live directing a broadcast, pulling in from multiple sources and making a show run smoothly? Quite possibly. But just like *September 5* from a few years ago, this movie really nails that feeling, that euphoria, when you have a story, and you are broadcasting it in a prestigious manner.

Once all these storylines finally come together, a lot of my earlier issues kind of go by the wayside. I am now drawn into what is going on, and I now do see its intention. My issue with *Disclosure Day* at large is the journey we have to go on to reach these moments. It's truly so rocky, from every line of dialogue being so vague to the fact that anytime a roadblock gets in the way of these characters accomplishing their goals, an act of God clears their path. This includes some of the most unintelligent police of all time simply making a wrong turn or not properly searching a crime scene, stormtrooper-like accuracy with firearms, or a car crashing in a very specific style that then holds up all other cops, giving our leads a platform to continue their run. This could be plot devices that come into play only when needed and are forgotten about in every other scenario, or, the worst offender of them all, characters just making the dumbest decisions that don't make sense in the moment and especially don't make sense down the line.

I am a massive fan of Steven Spielberg. That is partly why I'm a little disappointed with *Disclosure Day*. I was enraptured by Emily Blunt's phenomenal leading performance, and I was blown away by how the story ultimately wraps up, but there is a lack of any reason to care about these characters or the story at large, and, weirdly enough, the glimpses of Spielberg's artistic flair that popped out in certain sequences often felt mute throughout the rest of the runtime. If you told me this movie was directed by someone else, I would believe you for the majority of it. This does not have that Steven Spielberg artistic style dripped all over the production. This just feels like your run-of-the-mill sci-fi action movie with a few moments of greatness.

All in all, I know I will be in the minority. Leaving my press screening, I saw the people who cut in front of me in line talking to the agency rep about how much they loved this movie. One person, in fact, went as far as to say that this is their favorite Steven Spielberg movie to date, calling this the best film of his career. So clearly, there's going to be a range of opinions. While I may be more mixed, I have explained the aspects I really do adore and those that keep me at arm's length. You, the reader, can go see this movie, and maybe it becomes your new personal favorite Spielberg movie, or maybe you say it is his worst movie in over a decade.

That's the beauty about the art form. Not every LeBron James game was a masterclass. Not every MJ song was a classic. Greatness isn't measured by perfection; it's measured by the total body of work. *Disclosure Day* may ultimately land in the lower tier of Spielberg's filmography for me, but when your career includes decades of redefining what blockbuster filmmaking can be, one disappointment doesn't erase the résumé. It simply becomes another chapter.
