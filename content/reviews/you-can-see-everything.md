**“Why would I deceive you?”**

I swear I’m not going to spoil this movie. I will say nothing beyond what you likely already know from marketing, which I can tell you is effective at hiding many, many things. Thus, rather than beginning this review by discussing my appreciation of both filmmakers here, I’m going to talk about an entirely unrelated documentary that has literally nothing to do with *You Can See Everything*.

About a year ago now, a good friend of mine recommended I check out *Cameraperson*, a 2016 documentary by Kirsten Johnson. In this film, Johnson pieces together footage that she filmed across her entire career, whether they be sequences that were cut from various projects, home videos, or even just camera tests. Using this, she constructs a work of video with no temporal or locational consistency, simply flowing from idea to idea in a way that is hard to describe without actually viewing it. I was struck by the variety of concepts presented in this documentary and found watching it to be almost comforting, as if, through these snatches of moments, I was learning something more fundamental about the universe that we choose to constantly overlook. I’ve been dipping my toes into the documentary medium for the past few years (never enough, to be honest), but this was the first work to really take my preconstructed schema of what a documentary means and rattle the cages. There was a crack in the wall that I built in my mind to describe what a documentary means.

Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim have taken a sledgehammer to this wall and blown it wide open. I did not know you could possibly make a documentary like this.

I think it’s really important to emphasize Oppenheim’s name on this project, as while he is probably the less well-known name of the two, his fingerprints are truly all over this. I’m almost drawn to call this more of an Oppenheim and Fielder documentary than a Fielder and Oppenheim documentary, if that makes sense. Whether it’s the deliciously rich cinematography from David Bolen or the unnerving, hauntingly beautiful score from Ari Balouzian, this really does feel like a natural next step from the crew behind *Some Kind of Heaven*, *SPERMWORLD*, and the recently released *Primetime*. What makes all of these films stand out to me isn’t just their distinct technical aesthetic, but the ways in which their subject matter complicates the initial feelings the viewer might have upon seeing them. They never go the way you expect, but not in a rug-pulling way; more so a slow descent into madness as you spiral out with these wacky people.

Having said all of that, it’s unquestionable that this documentary wouldn’t quite sing at the level it does without the business school graduate himself. Nathan Fielder inexorably links himself with this documentary, whether it’s through his delightfully awkward persona or the fascinating ways in which he cares to investigate the psychology of humanity. He helps some of these scenes prove laugh-out-loud uproarious, with some of the best comedy I’ve seen all year, but also brings a profound complication to the narrative that got my heart racing and my mental gears turning at the same time. As an enjoyer of *Nathan for You* and a deep lover of *The Rehearsal*, it’s always hard to know how intentional everything is from him. However, the consistency of some themes really does convince me that the man has such fascinatingly distinct ideas about the human condition, and the ways in which *You Can See Everything* represents these ideas are unlike anything he’s ever done before.

This is the part where I basically just start saying that I can’t say what I want to say because of spoilers. I truly do not want you to know where this is going to go. What I can say is that I really do believe that the way this film evolves will not be what you expect. It doesn’t matter how well you know Fielder or Oppenheim; they’ve got tricks in the bag that you aren’t ready for, and they’re going to hit. There’s no two ways about it. The food for thought this film left me with, the hugely weighty thematic concepts explored in ways I’ve never seen before or presented in ways I’d never even thought of before, the trajectory along which this documentary travels… I’ve never quite felt this way watching any movie before. It’s left me feeling a distinct way ever since I came home, ever since I even started writing this review.

This is a profound, moving, hilarious, painful, unbelievable work of art. I’ve seen a fair number of documentaries and deeply love a few, including the aforementioned *Cameraperson*. Nevertheless, I think I’m already comfortable putting this one on top of the pile. You know how some people come out of some movies and say, “They don’t make them like this anymore”? I’m coming out of this saying that they’ve *never* made them like this before, and I don’t know if they ever will again.
