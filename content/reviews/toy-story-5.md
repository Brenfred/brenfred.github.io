With new films released over the course of thirty years, several generations have grown up enthralled by the Toy Story franchise. What kid wouldn't love to imagine what their toys get up to when they're not being played with? It's an evergreen idea that keeps on giving, and this latest installment keeps that spirit alive.

Feelings of obsolescence, the pain of being replaced, existential dread… these themes have been present in this franchise since the very beginning, and this trend continues in the fifth installment. Bonnie, our titular toys' current owner, is not like the other kids, and she is old enough now that she has become painfully aware of this. All of the other kids on her block are obsessed with the latest trend: the Lilypad, essentially a starter version of a tablet marketed to kids. Bonnie still plays with toys, causing her peers to treat her as an outcast. It's a heartbreaking depiction of the all-too-familiar way children can easily exclude anyone seen as different. Her parents worry about her struggle to make friends, so they give in and finally buy her a Lilypad. This new character, voiced by Greta Lee, immediately becomes a huge problem for the toys, as she sucks up all of Bonnie's time and leaves them neglected. It's up to Jessie, as the new sheriff in Woody's absence, to come up with a plan to save the toys from the dreaded garage.

Although Woody arrives on the scene later in the film and Buzz has a character arc of his own, this story truly belongs to Jessie. Since she was introduced as the breakout star of *Toy Story 2*, Jessie has always been a compelling character, and she is done right here with a deeper exploration of her past and how that informs her feelings in the present. While Woody was a great leader, his pride is an ever-present character flaw, causing him setbacks he must overcome several times throughout the series. Jessie, however, is presented here as a selfless leader, willing to take whatever risk necessary to protect Bonnie. There is a lovely reflection here on a toy's role in helping a child through a few crucial years in their life. After all, the toys of Toy Story take on the roles of parents in more ways than one. The "When She Loved Me" sequence from *Toy Story 2* starring Jessie and her first kid, Emily, still stands today as one of the most beautiful scenes in Pixar's entire storied history. This scene is called back here in a way that allows Jessie to experience closure and reconcile with her feelings of being left behind and not being good enough.

Another way this entry follows in the footsteps of its forebears is by introducing a plethora of new characters. The highlight of these is Conan O'Brien's Smarty Pants, a battery-powered potty-training toy that has had to deal with the obsolescence of his own, as his kid no longer needs him. It can feel a bit over-stuffed at times, as this franchise has accumulated so many characters over the years that some of them are reduced to a few precious moments. There is a questionable subplot involving a large shipment of new Buzz Lightyear toys trying to make their way to Star Command, but it ultimately pays off in an exciting way that calls back to some of the best moments from previous films. Although some characters, both old and new, are underserved here, it is a great experience just being able to spend time with our old friends again.

While this film may not hit the same highs that previous Toy Story films have, it is a satisfying and heartfelt exploration of the challenges children face growing up in a digital era. Focusing on Jessie and introducing technology as the primary antagonist are both smart moves in order to keep this story fresh. After all, these toys are over thirty years old now: changes were bound to happen. Technology and culture may have accelerated at a rapid pace in this century, but the Toy Story films have survived through it all, and they continue to capture the imaginations of children and adults alike. This latest entry is no exception, sure to delight audiences for years to come.
