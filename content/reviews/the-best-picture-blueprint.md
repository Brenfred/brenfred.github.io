As Oscar enthusiasts, we all strive to get that perfect "10 for 10" Best Picture lineup as early in the season as possible. Some say predicting awards before we hit the fall season is pointless because you don't know anything about these movies. "No one has seen these films, so what are you even predicting based on?" Now, we might have no intel to use, but that's exactly where awards season enthusiasts and addicts like us thrive. Anyone can make predictions when you have seen precursors and know which films have a 90 on Metacritic and which made a bajillion dollars at the box office, but where the real fun of predicting comes from is when you're flying blind. All you have is word-of-mouth rumors, all you have is looking at filmmakers' past track records and experiences…

But what if I told you there's another avenue to help perfect your Best Picture predictions early in the season? Because while, yes, you can look at filmmakers, studios, or even the actors involved in a project, one aspect I think is severely underrated in awards-season prognostication is *where* Best Picture nominees premiere throughout the year. With the Cannes Film Festival just recently wrapping up, we've heard about so many great titles that we are all looking forward to, but how many of these films truly contend for a Best Picture nomination? We saw last year that Neon had three, potentially even four contenders out of Cannes, but they only managed two in the end. If we look further back, since 2021, the Cannes Film Festival has routinely gotten at least one, if not two or three, Best Picture nominees each year. So I wanted to craft this article examining the major platforms where films can premiere throughout the year and how often each one leads to a Best Picture nomination.

Before we dive into the stats, just to give some background, this analysis will be focused on the period from the 2021 Oscar season, when **CODA** stormed the race at the last minute, upsetting **The Power of the Dog** at SAG, PGA, and, of course, the Oscars. I've decided to start my research from 2021 because that was the first year of the fixed 10-film Best Picture lineup, and because the pre-pandemic awards landscape was completely different than today's. Specifically, many people point to **Parasite**'s historic win in 2019 as broadening the Academy's taste for international features and for allowing more types of cinema and genre films to be represented at the Academy Awards.

The first type of premiere that we'll be covering is the **Sundance Film Festival**. Although small in size, this festival can often pack an emotional punch with its major contenders. Over the last few years, we've seen films like **CODA** make the run from the beginning of the season to win Best Picture, but also films like **Train Dreams**, where most predictors don't take them seriously until critics groups. Next, we will be looking at the **Cannes Film Festival**. Cannes is arguably the most important festival in the modern era, in large part because of **Parasite**'s historic victory. Every season since has produced at least one Best Picture nominee, and oftentimes more. Then we'll look at films released theatrically before September. These are films that do not premiere at film festivals and are often major blockbusters that hit the cultural zeitgeist and remain in the conversation throughout the year.

As we dive into the fall, we have the **Venice Film Festival**. Pre-pandemic, one could say this was the most integral film festival for Academy Awards prospects, but with Cannes' emergence, Venice has taken a backseat. However, Venice has remained quite consistent in producing Best Picture nominees. Next, we have various North American film festivals. These include **Telluride**, **Toronto International Film Festival (TIFF)**, and the **New York Film Festival (NYFF)**. This may be surprising to some of you, but Telluride has recently statistically surpassed TIFF as a platform for premieres, even though TIFF remains more culturally relevant. NYFF is a fest that many Oscar prognosticators liken to a death sentence for world premieres. And finally, we have films released theatrically between September and December. These films are generally major holiday spectacles and films that attempt to make a major splash right before the nomination period opens.

Looking at this year's crop of Best Picture contenders, we could take the statistical breakdown of where our past five years of Best Picture nominees have premiered and try to build our Best Picture lineup based on this. Based on our established averages, Sundance is getting between 0–1 Best Picture nominees per year, leaving films like **The Invite** or **Josephine** at risk of missing Best Picture.

Moving along to the Cannes Film Festival, 2–3 films that premiere alongside La Croisette make our Best Picture lineup, the highest of any category. However, when looking at the outlook of this year's edition, I'm leaning towards two instead of three. While films like **All of a Sudden** and **Fatherland** have great critical reception, they also appear to be missing some of that extra passion we've seen for films like the Palme d'Or winner, **Fjord**, and **The Black Ball**. Additionally, those two films are both the presumed #1 priority of a proven distributor, belonging to Neon and Netflix, respectively, while All of a Sudden and Fatherland do not share this distinction.

Also, there is one out-of-competition title to note, being **Club Kid**. A24 purchased the film for a reported $21 million, a steep price tag that shows confidence in the movie, but I'm still taking a "wait and see" stance on the film.

Over the last five years, six films released before the fall film festivals have made Best Picture, and this year we have a good batch of contenders. Obviously, we have already seen **Project Hail Mary**, but we still have the likes of **The Odyssey** and **Disclosure Day** on the horizon, and there are even some who still believe that **Michael** could sneak into a Best Picture lineup of 10.

Looking ahead to our fall festival slate, Venice looks absolutely stacked, and, as detailed above, it consistently pulls in two Best Picture nominees. This year, there's the potential for films like **The Adventures of Cliff Booth**, **Cry to Heaven**, **Jack of Spades**, **Possible Love**, **A Place in Hell**, and **Wild Horse Nine**, all to premiere at the festival. Even still, that doesn't include films like **Behemoth!** or **Digger**, which I think have alternative paths to premiering.

The Telluride Film Festival has been a sleeper hit with the Academy lately. Over the last two years, they have pulled in at least 2 Best Picture nominees, and this year they could follow suit. I would expect films like **Behemoth!**, **Saturn Return**, and **Sense and Sensibility** to premiere at the festival, and there's even a chance Telluride could steal some TIFF premieres.

Speaking of TIFF, it has been on a downward trajectory recently, only racking up 0–1 nominees per year in this span. I currently see **Being Heumann**, **Misty Green**, **No One Cares**, **Prima Facie**, **Primetime**, **The Social Reckoning**, and **Wildwood** as our shiny TIFF premieres competing for the illustrious PCA slots.

The New York Film Festival has historically just not been a path for potential Best Picture nominees, with only **Marty Supreme** making the cut. We have seen the festival be a failed launching pad for films such as **Is This Thing On?**, **She Said**, **Till**, and **The Tragedy of Macbeth** over the last few years. That leads us into all the theatrical releases after August. The stats say that, on average, 1–2 films make it into Best Picture, but those numbers are heavily skewed by 2021, when four post-August releases made it into Best Picture. Recently, we've been seeing only one or two post-August films make the cut, and, given reports that Warner Bros. is no longer interested in the festival game, I think Alejandro González Iñárritu's **Digger** and Denis Villeneuve's **Dune: Part Three** emerge as strong contenders to fill this slot, but another possible contender includes Robert Eggers' **Werwulf**.

So what were all the stats for? For you, the reader, to formulate your own opinion and to craft your Best Picture lineup with the knowledge of where films typically premiere, of course. But also, it's to help me make sure I'm formulating the best possible Best Picture lineup I could here in the early summer months after the Cannes Film Festival.

So, taking my own advice, here are the 10 films I am currently predicting for Best Picture and where they premiered, using the stats. I currently have the trend of Sundance having a BP nominee in alternating years continuing for 2026, with both **The Invite** and **Josephine** missing out on my Best Picture lineup.

For the Cannes Film Festival, I'm predicting a slight down year, with only two festival titles making Best Picture: **The Black Ball** and **Fjord**. While yes, we have had a string of years that saw three films make Best Picture, those years were all heavily skewed toward English-language titles. In 2022, we had both Elvis and Top Gun: Maverick, not to mention that Triangle of Sadness is predominantly in English, even though it is a European production. In 2023, we had Killers of the Flower Moon alongside Anatomy of a Fall and The Zone of Interest. In 2024, Anora, Emilia Pérez, and The Substance. I hear you — Emilia Pérez is largely in Spanish, but at the end of the day, it's a production featuring American stars. The Substance may have been made by a French filmmaker, but it has a Hollywood cast, and of course, Anora is an American indie darling. Then, last year, we dropped to just two Best Picture nominees, and that's because both are international productions, with The Secret Agent and Sentimental Value. Sure, Sentimental Value does have an American in the cast and names that are familiar to Hollywood, but at its core, it is a European film. There was no extra American title to help boost these numbers to three. So this year, without that extra American title, I am only going with two Cannes films.

While I may be on the low side for Cannes, I am on the high side for films released before the fall film festivals. First, I've gone with **Project Hail Mary**, which has already been released and is likely to earn a few tech nominations and could even snag multiple above-the-line nominations. Of course, my second is Christopher Nolan's **The Odyssey**. Some could argue that it's the current Best Picture frontrunner, and who am I to deny Nolan his second round of Oscar glory?

Now, over our observation period, Venice has produced two Best Picture nominees every year, so, logically, I've also gone with two. **Wild Horse Nine** is by far the most obvious choice on the table here, but the race for the second slot is wide open. **Cry to Heaven** is a fairly popular pick among the wider awards community, currently sitting at #8 on Awards Expert. However, I'm a little hesitant because we don't have an official distributor for the movie yet, and the same could be said for Joel Coen's **Jack of Spades**. Jack of Spades could also premiere at another festival. Coen's other solo directorial project, **The Tragedy of Macbeth**, premiered at NYFF, which, as we have already established, would place it on an uphill path. **A Place in Hell** sounds interesting, but I'm still not quite sold on it as a Best Picture contender, which leaves us with Netflix. The streaming giant could premiere both **The Adventures of Cliff Booth** and **Possible Love**. Obviously, Netflix has gone hard for The Black Ball out of Cannes, suggesting that it's Netflix's main international title. As such, I'm leaning towards **The Adventures of Cliff Booth**, which, shockingly, is kind of a hot take. I mean, it's a film directed by David Fincher, written by Quentin Tarantino, and is a continuation of the Once Upon a Time in Hollywood lore. Even if this doesn't make Best Picture, I'm envisioning a Glass Onion-type situation where Netflix will try its hardest, especially given the premium release date and IMAX screens.

Telluride has also been on the up and up over the last few years, consistently producing multiple Best Picture nominees, and I think the same will happen this year. I think Searchlight's **Behemoth!** directed by Tony Gilroy and Focus Features' **Sense and Sensibility**, directed by Georgia Oakley, both look strong on paper. I could see these two going to TIFF instead, but if they are legitimately Best Picture contenders, I feel like Telluride would be a better launching pad.

So if I have Cannes, pre-September theatrical, Venice, and Telluride each getting two, I only have two Best Picture slots left, which means I am continuing the trend of TIFF not having a world premiere in the Best Picture lineup. I do believe that titles like **The Adventures of Cliff Booth**, **Behemoth!**, **Sense and Sensibility**, and **Wild Horse Nine** will also screen at TIFF, but they just will not be world premieres.

New York? Do we even have to talk about it?

And that leaves me with two Best Picture slots remaining for all films released theatrically after August, which is where Warner Bros. comes into play. Following in the footsteps of their Best Picture winner last year, **One Battle After Another**, I believe that Alejandro González Iñárritu's **Digger** will skip the fall festivals. And, of course, we have our major December release of **Dune: Part Three**. IMAX tickets have already gone on sale and sold out instantly. The hype is there, and I believe that Dune can pull off what Avatar could not and have all three installments become Best Picture nominees.
