In 2019, the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences changed the name of the "Best Foreign-Language Film" category to "Best International Film." It was a symbolic shift that showed a general interest in all sorts of films from outside the US border, but also implied a widening field that would include films from other majority English-speaking countries. In effect, it changed nothing. Countries each need to organize themselves to create a committee and officially submit one film to contend for a nomination in this category. The rules stayed the same.

The Oscars are still the only major awards body to handle the category in this way, and while it narrows the field to a manageable number of films coming from all over the world, it also opens a whole other can of worms. Should it be up to the countries to organize and decide which of their films in a given year should represent them? Is it fair for major film-exporting countries to only have one film in the race? And why is Luxembourg, a country of around 700k citizens, granted as many submissions as India, a country of 1.5 billion? So while the Academy has made changes to the rules of the international film award, such as the executive "save" rule (discontinued) or the expansion of the shortlist from 9 to 15, they feel like small potatoes compared to the Herculean task of making the category fair.

This year marks the first time that the Academy has made a major addition to the rules of the submission system since the creation of the category in 1955. For the first time in the category's history, a film doesn't need to be submitted by its country of origin to contend for the award; it can also qualify in the extraordinarily rare case where it wins the jury-awarded top prize of a designated international film festival competition. There are currently 6 festivals designated to have that power including Sundance, Berlin, Cannes, Venice, TIFF, and Busan, which adds a maximum of 7 additional films in the race per year (yes, because Sundance gets two from both its world cinema sections). The lucky crop of festival winners this year are *Shame and Money*, *To Hold a Mountain*, *Yellow Letters*, *Fjord*, *Woman Unknown*, *Angh*, and *Midday Meal* respectively.

One of the headlines this year was the record-breaking number of country submissions at 101 (up by 8 from 2022's 93 submissions). But adding the festival-qualifying films only lifts the total number to 105. Turns out, countries are free to submit films that have already qualified by winning a top festival prize, and seem more than happy to do so as a way to take credit for the given film. Denmark, for example, submitted *Woman Unknown* a week after it had won the Golden Lion, instead of going for one of their other two shortlisted films, *Home* and *What Lies Beneath Us*. They simply went for the film that had the best odds of performing well in the category, regardless of whether it had already qualified or not. Ditto for Romania and Montenegro, although the latter may not have had many other choices.

As for the other four films, it's a mixed bag. *Yellow Letters* got really lucky being a high-profile international film (from the nominated director of *The Teacher's Lounge*) which released in a strong and competitive year for Germany. The country was much more likely to submit films like *Everytime*, *Rose*, or *Gentle Monster* (all four films were on the national shortlist). A film like *Shame and Money*, directed by a previously submitted filmmaker, was lucky to qualify and give Kosovo the easy decision of submitting Cannes Critics' Week highlight *Dua* instead.

As for the other two, things aren't looking as bright. Sure, India was incredibly unlikely to ever submit *Angh*, but having premiered in mid-September in a competitive program with little news coverage did it no favors. Despite positive early ratings, awareness for the film is so low that it feels doomed from the start. Things might be even worse for *Midday Meal*, having won the Busan competition (a program 2 years young in a festival with little North American awareness). This is coming from director Majid Majidi, who is an Iran submission staple. While his contemporaries like Jafar Panahi, Saeed Roustaee, and Mohammad Rasoulof make films deemed too incendiary for the regime, Majidi's films don't ruffle feathers. He helped Iran get its first nomination in 1998 and was submitted multiple times, as recently as 2020 with *Sun Children* (which was shortlisted). If next year, Iran had nothing better to submit than this well-reviewed gem, they surely would've sent it to the Oscars. But alas, winning the top prize of this little-known competition program in mid-October thrusts the film into a race in which it doesn't have enough of a runway to take off.

Arguably, this rule change was implemented to address some major snubs in the last few years. In the same way that the executives' "save" rule was a response to Cristian Mungiu's *4 Months 3 Weeks 2 Days* being inexplicably snubbed from the shortlist, the Academy surely wanted to avoid leaving films like *Anatomy of a Fall* out of the race entirely. Looking at this decade, major films like *All That Breathes* (Sundance '22), *There Is No Evil* (Berlin '20), *Dreams* (Berlin '25), *Happening* (Venice '21), *Riceboy Sleeps* (TIFF '22), and the aforementioned *Anatomy of a Fall* (Cannes '23) would've been thrown into the race. I would argue that all of these had a genuine chance of at least making the shortlist of 15 (although *Riceboy Sleeps* would've benefitted from waiting until 2023).

But what about a film like *Two Seasons, Two Strangers*? That film won the Golden Leopard, the Locarno Film Festival's top prize. That's a major European festival that isn't currently Academy-qualifying. Should it not be considered?

And what about a film like 2025's *A Useful Ghost* or this year's *La Gradiva*? Those films won the top prize of the Cannes Critics' Week Competition and are some of the most talked-about international films of their years. Will it be too messy to start also attributing Academy qualifications to sidebars and other programs like Un Certain Regard (which could've qualified *Black Dog* in 2024)? What about all the unsubmitted films from Directors' Fortnight, which is an unjuried selection? Are films like *Hit the Road* and *Falcon Lake* doomed?

Then we have films like *All We Imagine as Light*, notoriously snubbed by India. This new rule would fall just short of saving it, as it won the Grand Prix (2nd place) to *Anora*'s Palme d'Or. And while we're talking about India, what about *RRR*? An internationally celebrated film that opted for a traditional theatrical rollout and played no major festivals. Is it impossible to save it?

You gotta draw the line somewhere; otherwise, the Academy would have to manage too many films, but how much is too much? Looking back to this year specifically, this rule helped 4 films qualify independently of their country of production. It remains to be seen whether any of them can muster an effective campaign and find their way onto the Academy shortlist. But if the predicted standings of the Filmball community stand, none of them will remain in the race by mid-December. Is it because of the films themselves, because of a lack of support from national cultural institutions, or plain bad luck? We can't know for sure. But what is clear is that this year just did not yield the results that this new rule hoped it would.

Sure, in very specific circumstances, one or two major films can be saved through this rule, but there are still too many major omissions left unconsidered. This year, the aforementioned *La Gradiva* will sit out the Oscar race despite being unanimously celebrated, but so will a high-profile film like *Gentle Monster*, a festival gem like *Moscas*, and a popular blockbuster like *Godzilla Minus Zero*.

The best thing about this new rule is that there is now an objective way for films to jump into the race (or rather, one determined by different factors). Some countries are notorious censors through omission, whether it's politically motivated or just in bad taste, and this rule, for what it's worth, bypasses national submission committees. If the Academy can find a way to have dissident filmmakers (or voices disliked in their country of origin) systematically included in the race, it would be a real achievement. Easier said than done.

If the Academy plans on expanding on this new rule (as opposed to scrapping it), we can only hope that they help the films emerging from the most fruitful competitive programs. Hopefully, they can veto a second submission for India, assuring that S.S. Rajamouli's new film qualifies, offsetting Luxembourg's current advantage in the process. C'mon, Academy, you got one year to make this happen!
