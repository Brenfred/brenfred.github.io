**“I saw myself…”**

Jane Schoenbrun is, in my opinion, one of the most vibrantly distinct filmmakers working today. They’ve already achieved a mastery of underscoring the liminality of life, exploring the dark depths of the media which consumes our everyday life, and investigating the queering of pop culture to a level I’ve never seen from anyone else before. Each of their prior two feature films has left me silent, deeply pondering the ramifications of their motifs and generally settling over me like a slimy blanket of weirdness. Something about their films gets under my skin in a unique way, and I walked into *Teenage Sex and Death at Camp Miasma* expecting nothing less.

This film is not *We’re All Going to the World’s Fair*, and it’s not *I Saw the TV Glow*. This is a maximalist, messy, overwhelming, heartfelt nightmare of a roller coaster that left me struggling for breath by the end. Eternally indebted to a long history of slashers yet so original that I struggle to imagine any other film even remotely coming close to what this is. Schoenbrun hasn’t just hit another home run; they’ve evolved.

The ways in which this film subverts expectations were beyond what I could’ve anticipated. I was prepared for a love letter to slashers, and this is decidedly that, but it’s a TRUE love letter in the most literal sense of the word. It isn’t just the romantic parts of a love letter, but the obsessive nature of them, the neediness and undercurrent of toxic codependency that underscores the most fervently desperate of notes of longing. The passion for the slasher genre feels so all-encompassing that it swallows the film whole, turning it into something else; a film so fixated on a subgenre that it becomes a deconstruction of it at the same time. This language all sounds flowery and abstract, but I struggle to even conceptualize how to describe this using concrete terms. It needs to be seen to be believed.

Naturally, from a technical perspective, nothing but praise can be levied. The stunning photography from Eric K. Yue creates one of the most well-crafted visual identities of the year, backed by a stirring and emotional score from Alex G. This film is deeply well cast from top to bottom, with wondrous supporting and background performances everywhere you turn your head, but of course the beating heart of the film lies within the subtle madness in the eyes of our two primary characters. Hannah Einbinder stars striking a formidable balance playing a role that feels simultaneously grounded and larger than life, and Gillian Anderson shines in every scene she’s in with raw pain and the unequivocal confidence of an actress who knows they can steal any scene they walk into. An underrated standout here is the work of Amanda Fix, who is tasked with perhaps the most important moment of performance in the whole film, and delivers with shining colors. This cast builds a tapestry of wonder from which there’s no looking away.

Schoenbrun’s vision is the star of the show, and it is here where I hesitate to lavish unadulterated praise. To be honest, a small piece of me wonders if the utter lack of restraint went just a hair over the line, where at times it feels as if the film is almost getting away from the filmmaker and taking on a life of its own. There’s an addictive, engaging quality to this, as I couldn’t help but gasp with delight at the directions where this movie took me, but by the end I felt so burdened with the weight of my experience that just trying to process it all has proven a daunting task. This is definitely a film that would benefit from rewatches, and I could imagine this small quibble going away with ease, but for now it is the one thing holding me ever so slightly back from unabashed acclaim.

Nevertheless, I cannot emphasize enough how fresh and bold this work is for Schoenbrun. It feels like they’ve unlocked a whole new layer through which they can peel back the fabric of our reality. Rather than losing us in the liminality, they grab us by the shoulders and thrust us headfirst into a horrifying, gorgeous world built from the bones of all the DVDs they must’ve loved to watch when younger. This is a joy, a scream, and an attack on every sense. I’ve already mentioned that it must be seen to be believed, but really what it would be is experienced.

I realize this review has been hard to pin down, and I apologize for the metaphorical vagueness. I’ll end with a concrete and direct call to action. If you love horror, if you love queerness, if you love film, or if you love analyzing the very idea of love, this is for you. Let the tsunami of watching it crash over you as it did me.
