Pedro Almodóvar isn't one to shy away from inserting himself into his films; his last universally acclaimed film, *Pain and Glory*, is mostly autobiographical. But in *Bitter Christmas*, the Spanish auteur takes it one step further, essentially making a film about himself writing the very film we're watching. And though the themes of inspiration and the ethics of "writing what you know" only really click into gear in the film's last act, they are undoubtedly fascinating.

*Bitter Christmas* follows two narratives. The first, about Raúl (Leonardo Sbaraglia), a legendary film director proxy for Almodóvar himself, writing a screenplay to pull himself out of his creative block and make a comeback into narrative filmmaking. The second narrative is the screenplay itself, about Elsa (Bárbara Lennie), a filmmaker suffering from panic attacks as she copes with the death of her mother. Things begin to take a turn for Raúl when he decides to incorporate a real-life tragedy his long-time assistant, Mónica (Aitana Sánchez-Gijón), is coping with into his screenplay, causing friction between Mónica and himself.

The ethics explored here are quite interesting. Should creatives have total freedom to use real people's lives for the sake of their art, or are there lines they should not cross? And if there are, are these boundaries determined by the greatness of the artist? It reminds me of the dilemma explored in Dostoevsky's *Crime and Punishment*, about whether superior men and women are bound by the same moral rules as the rest of us. In the case of Raúl, who's meant to be a great artist, he feels it's the best screenplay he's written in years, but it only works if he gets free reign to use Mónica's life in whatever way his narrative requires. Do the ethics of allowing great art to be created outweigh the emotional damage its creation may cause to others? Or, specifically, to the artist's loved ones?

What holds these themes back from fully landing is that, in my opinion, the screenplay being written by Raúl isn't nearly as great as Almodóvar pretends it is. In fact, much of *Bitter Christmas* as a whole lacks narrative thrust, and it feels like we're just jumping between these two narratives without much interesting going on in either of them. The film really only clicks into gear when we reach the end of the screenplay, which is when Raúl decides to include Mónica's personal life tragedy as a sort of epilogue. At that point, it's hard not to feel like we've been waiting an hour and a half watching a subpar Almodóvar film before the good stuff begins.

Fortunately, everything fans have come to expect from his films are there. The colourful mise-en-scène that makes every setting look like a work of art in itself, the riveting string score, the complex female protagonist, the dark comedy. There are two stand-out scenes in Raúl's screenplay — a hilarious male striptease and a powerful rendition of Chavela García's "Las simples cosas" sung to Elsa by her bedside — that belong up there with Almodóvar's finest moments. Had more of *Bitter Christmas* been at that level, maybe I wouldn't have minded the introspective themes arriving so late in the runtime.

As far as awards prospects are concerned, *Bitter Christmas* got fairly middling reviews out of Cannes. It's going to TIFF, where it may hope to find a lifeline of some sort, but it seems like a lost cause, especially given the success of *La Bola Negra*, which will almost certainly be Spain's submission for International Feature. There's a new rule in which an international film may still compete for International Feature without being a country's official submission by winning a top prize at one of the top film festivals. If either *La Bola Negra* or *Bitter Christmas* wins at TIFF, Spain could opt to submit the other, to allow potentially two Spanish films into the category. This seems very unlikely, though, and even if it were to happen, I just don't see the Academy bringing Almodóvar back into the lineup for a film that isn't considered a standout of his filmography. *The Room Next Door* won the Golden Lion at Venice two years ago, and even that wasn't enough for an Oscar nomination.

Time will tell how essential *Bitter Christmas* is to the filmmaker's oeuvre. Though the reviews currently seem pretty harsh, I have a feeling people will look back on it more fondly. Its self-referential nature makes it more interesting in the context of Almodóvar's filmography than as a standalone film, but even a subpar Almodóvar is worth watching for one reason or another. He's in his mid-70s, and though I haven't heard any talk of him slowing down, if this somehow turns out to be the last film he ever makes, I think it'd be an apt ending to his career, thematically and stylistically encapsulating much of what makes him such a singular voice in contemporary cinema.
