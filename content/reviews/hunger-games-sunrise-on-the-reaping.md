As a baby born in '99, I missed the window to be part of the camp that grew up reading *The Lord of the Rings* books and watching Peter Jackson's epic translation onto the big screen. As a dude in the late 2000s, my mom was not taking me to see *Twilight*. Instead, I was the perfect age demographic to grow up reading Suzanne Collins' *The Hunger Games* novels and watching their adaptations play out on the big screen.

But guess what? I don't like to read, so I never picked up *The Hunger Games* books. Honestly, I didn't even watch the movies until a few years ago, when the prequel, *The Ballad of Songbirds & Snakes*, was set to release in theaters in 2023. The trailers had me intrigued. As a fan of Rachel Zegler, I was interested in seeing what her next movie would be, and there was a certain bravado Tom Blyth brought to the marketing that made me want to seek it out. So, to do my due diligence, I binge-watched the original four films starring Jennifer Lawrence before heading into *The Ballad of Songbirds & Snakes*, which, dare I say, might have been my favorite entry in the franchise up until that point. Maybe it was because I saw it in theaters, or because it felt so different from the Lawrence films, but something about that prequel really stuck with me (*Catching Fire* was great even though I watched it on a laptop, such epic arena sequences).

Fast-forward to 2026, and I have thought every piece of marketing for *The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping* has been fantastic. From the early trailer launch alongside *Wicked: For Good* to each new trailer instantly hooking me with the countdown and the shots of the first few seconds in the new arena, the stylish character posters, and fun cast interviews, my excitement for the newest entry in the franchise has only continued to grow until right now…. With my immediate reaction walking out of the theater being… "Yeah, this is a GREAT *Hunger Games* movie".

My expectations for *Sunrise on the Reaping* were sky-high, and while they weren't entirely met, I wouldn't necessarily call myself disappointed. In fact, I think that's a testament to the film. *Sunrise on the Reaping* is a great time that delivers exactly what you want out of a *Hunger Games* movie. It has the performances, it has the spectacle, it has the arena sequences, and it has the emotional gut punches that have become synonymous with the franchise. But despite all of that, it never quite goes above and beyond what you might expect. So let's talk about it.

Starting with the biggest draw, and arguably the reason audiences are going to flock to theaters this weekend, *Sunrise on the Reaping* finally puts Haymitch Abernathy front and center. Made famous by Woody Harrelson's iconic portrayal in the original four films, this time around we're introduced to a 16-year-old Haymitch, played by newcomer Joseph Zada, as he enters the arena for the Second Quarter Quell.

When the cast announcement first happened, and even during the initial trailers, you looked at Zada and didn't immediately see Woody Harrelson. From his young, model-like looks to his blonde curls and boyish nature, he wasn't exactly who you pictured when thinking about Haymitch. But that's what makes his performance so impressive. Before we even officially enter the arena, Zada has already convinced you that you're watching a younger version of the character we've come to know and love. Zada absolutely nails the voice, but even more impressively, he captures the mannerisms. There's an energy, optimism, and youthful confidence to his interpretation that makes it easy to understand who Haymitch was before the world took everything away from him and as the story unfolds, you begin to see those cracks form and witness the inevitable transformation into the Haymitch we already know.

It's a fantastic breakout performance, and one that should put Zada on the radar of just about every casting director in Hollywood.

But he's far from the only standout in the movie because one of my favorite aspects of *Sunrise on the Reaping* is how this star-studded ensemble brings their own interpretations to these iconic characters without simply mimicking the performances that came before. You can recognize where certain inspirations come from, and there are moments where creative liberties are clearly being taken, but this cast manages to make these characters feel familiar while still giving them something new. Whether it's Zada's Haymitch, Elle Fanning's charismatic Effie, Kelvin Harrison Jr. nailing the voice of Beetee, Ralph Fiennes' stoic President Snow, or Jesse Plemons' stealing scenes as Plutarch, you have to marvel at the casting and what this ensemble pulls off.

There is, however, one performance that didn't quite work for me: Academy Award winner Kieran Culkin as Caesar Flickerman. To put it lightly, this Caesar feels like he walked onto set and said, "Screw the source material. I'm gonna do my own thing." Compared to Stanley Tucci's iconic portrayal, Culkin's interpretation feels like an entirely different character. His delivery, mannerisms, and overall energy never quite clicked for me. It's a strange choice in a movie where so many other actors manage to find that balance between honoring what came before and making the role their own.

On the flip side, Plemons continues to show why he's one of the brightest stars in all of Hollywood. Taking inspiration from Philip Seymour Hoffman's portrayal of Plutarch in the original films while injecting his own mannerisms and creativity into the role, Plemons is another fantastic addition to an already stacked ensemble.

Of course, the other major draw of *Sunrise on the Reaping* is getting to see the iconic Second Quarter Quell play out on the big screen. As stated in the film, "presentation is everything" and *Sunrise on the Reaping* makes you feel it to your core. Instead of two tributes per district, we're now looking at four. Instead of 24 tributes entering the arena, there are 48. The stakes are higher, the arena is bigger, and there's no shortage of opportunities for the franchise to deliver the kind of spectacle audiences have come to expect.

I especially enjoyed the visual identity of the arena sequences. Having the opportunity to see this in IMAX made the experience even more rewarding. Outside the arena, the film maintains its standard visual format, but once we step onto the battlegrounds, the image expands to fill the full IMAX ratio. Is it as world-shattering as the barn door sequence in *Sinners*? Not quite. But you don't need a flashy visual gimmick to appreciate what's happening on screen.

The expanded presentation gives the arena a distinct identity, while the sound design pulls you directly into the chaos of the competition. The eye-popping colors and overall production design also help differentiate this environment from the arenas we've seen throughout the franchise. It feels like a fresh setting with its own personality, and the craft team does a great job of making the most of it. More importantly, I found most of the action sequences to deliver. There's an intensity to the competition that makes you appreciate just how dangerous this version of the Games really is. While it doesn't match *Catching Fire*'s insane action, it is the best we have seen since.

Now, do I wish we got to spend more time with the other tributes? Absolutely. Would I have liked to see more tribute v tribute combat and a little more time exploring the sheer chaos of having 48 people in the arena? Of course. That might make me sound like one of the violence enthusiasts coming out of District 1, but it's hard not to want more when the movie sets up such an interesting premise.

At the same time, I appreciate the more character-focused story we ultimately get. Rather than making the entire movie about the spectacle of the Second Quarter Quell, *Sunrise on the Reaping* spends much of its time diving deeper into Haymitch and the people who shape his life. Watching a young man enter this competition full of optimism and love but he slowly loses everything he holds dear is an incredibly effective way to explore the character. It's not just about watching someone survive the Hunger Games; it's about understanding the person who walks out of that arena and the emotional repercussions that follow. The focus on how to break the machine and what breaking it does to one's core gives the story a different kind of weight, even if it occasionally leaves you wanting more of the actual Games.

Ultimately, *Sunrise on the Reaping* delivers exactly what I want out of a *Hunger Games* movie. Joseph Zada is a revelation as young Haymitch, the ensemble is packed with fantastic performances, and the arena sequences provide the spectacle that makes this franchise so entertaining on the big screen. While I would have liked to spend more time with the other tributes and see the film push its arena sequences even further, I appreciate the decision to prioritize Haymitch's story and the emotional journey that comes with it.

If you're a fan of *The Hunger Games*, you're absolutely going to have a blast with this one. However, if you've never really been all that interested in the franchise, I don't think this is the entry that's going to win you over. It's a movie that knows exactly what it is and delivers on those expectations, even if it doesn't necessarily redefine what the franchise can be.

As someone who didn't grow up reading the books or following these movies from the beginning, it's been a lot of fun finding my way being won over by the world building over the last few years. *The Ballad of Songbirds & Snakes* helped turn me into a fan, and *Sunrise on the Reaping* gives me another reason to keep coming back. It might not be the franchise's greatest achievement, but it's another thoroughly entertaining trip to the theater, and one I'd absolutely encourage fans to experience on the biggest screen possible.
