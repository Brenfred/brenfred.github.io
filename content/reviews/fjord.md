**“Am I on trial for my beliefs?”**

I’ll be blunt when I say that writing a review of *Fjord* in a public setting is a terrifying prospect. The discourse war that feels inevitable when trying to engage with a real critique of this work is not an appealing prospect.

What I will say is that I think my own background coming into this film proved more important than I expected to inform how I viewed this. I would consider myself a remarkably leftist individual at this stage of my life, but I was raised in a religious household. I spent a lot of my childhood being taught conservative ideals, and it was only later in life that I really broke free of those concepts and developed my own philosophies, which skewed far more radical on the other side of the aisle. While I don’t intend to turn this review into an examination of my own political ideologies, I mention this for a key reason: I understand firsthand what it’s like to look at someone you disagree with fundamentally and see them as human. And I think that’s what Mungiu really wants to explore here.

*Fjord* is a legal drama about parents battling to get their kids back from protective custody. It feels almost straightforward when I describe the basic premise of what this film is about, and if you’re unfamiliar with how the story and characters unfold, you might almost be bored by the concept. What Mungiu is far more interested in here, though, is portraying a variety of complicated, problematic, and scarily sympathetic characters on both ends of the spectrum. This is a film written to provoke thought and to get a rise out of the viewer such that the viewer sits and wonders why a rise is being gotten out of them in the first place.

To dismiss this as conservative propaganda is an overzealous eschewal of the clearly complex intent in this screenplay and in the directorial choices being made. Mungiu isn’t encouraging everyone to root for a specific political side here; he wants you to consider what lies at the heart of oppression and what causes it fundamentally. This film argues that a lack of communication and a refusal towards understanding the mindset of those that think differently have led to complete polarization of ideologies, which has broken our ability to connect with others. *Fjord* is a film about how humans are still human, even if they do things you find abhorrent; this is a dismantling of the cartoonish villainization that *actual*propaganda does in favor of insisting that people be looked at with nuance.

This film would’ve fallen apart immediately if it was cast worse, and so credit must be given to the strength of the ensemble here. Stan and Reinsve are both doing solid work in their lead roles here; Reinsve is given a bit more to do and brings a bit more soul to the character that I found easier to engage with. I was pleasantly surprised to discover that I greatly enjoyed the performances of the children. I especially felt that the entire thematic conceit of this film’s impartiality relied on Henrikke Lund Olsen and Vanessa Ceban selling their characters in the first act; the fact that they succeed in this endeavor connects us with their characters, leaving us at a permanent limbo between frustration and feeling for the adults.

Panduru shoots this film with icy blues and distance that make it feel impossible to really reach out and grab the characters; you’re trapped watching everything without any ability to personally engage, simply forced into passive observation as this film tests the limits of your comfort in the theater. I also felt that the editing did a solid job of keeping this film moving at a fair clip; while I wouldn’t call this fast-paced, it never felt like it lost tempo, and I was sufficiently engaged for most of its runtime. Of course, the star of the show (for better or, for some, for worse) is the fascinating script from Mungiu that raises so many intellectual queries, and the ways in which his directorial choices refuse to give you answers. This is the most unsatisfying watch of the year, and there’s something incredibly engaging about that.

I don’t think this film is perfect. It runs a few minutes too long in my opinion, and I would’ve preferred if it slimmed out a subplot that I found didn’t do much to serve the story. I also feel that this film serves more as an intellectual thinkpiece than a truly gripping piece of cinema in the theater; this is honestly more engaging to discuss and write about than it is to watch. Nevertheless, it’s hard to deny that this is one of the most interesting films of the year. I implore those hesitant about what they’ve heard regarding the thematic content of this film to give it a shot and try to see it on its terms; I think you may be surprised by the depth of its commentary and the intelligence of its ideas.
