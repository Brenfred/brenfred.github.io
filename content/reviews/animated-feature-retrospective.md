I have a passionate, unadulterated love for animation. I think it's truly magical how worlds are brought to life through drawing, computer-generated imagery, clay, or other ingenious techniques, making us see a world that feels vibrant and distinct from our own yet still carries the same universal truths we can discover in live action. The Academy has grown to appreciate animation more over the course of the 21st century as well. While the Animated Feature category started out with a paltry three nominations for the year and unadulterated dominance from Disney and Pixar, recent years have seen a rise in exciting, distinct voices in the animation industry being recognized at a level never seen before.

As we enter what looks, from the outset, to be one of the most competitive animation years we've had in a while, I thought it prudent to look back on the past few years, analyze some trends, and even put together a couple of graphs for fun.

For the purposes of this analysis, we will be limiting our focus to the 45 Animated Feature nominees from the year of 2017 to the year of 2025. Why 2017, you ask? Simply put, this was the first year in which the voting rules for this category changed: rather than the animation branch voting alone, nominees from then on were voted on by the entire Academy. This, I believe, has led to clear differentiation in both nomination patterns and winning patterns in the past few years, and also maps nicely onto examining what the category looks like in a post-Disney/Pixar dominant era, a more international-leaning era, and a generally distinct Academy that has developed in the past decade.

<h2>Are the nominees any good?</h2>

First off, let's look at typical reception levels for animated nominees. This is going to be a lot of "well, obviously" with few surprises, but it's good to set some baseline expectations, and also grants me the opportunity to make graphs in Google Sheets, which I simply couldn't resist.

I've charted out our 45 nominees by Letterboxd score, IMDb score, Rotten Tomatoes average critic rating, and Metacritic score. The former two give a reasonable sense of broader audience reception, while the latter two give a reasonable sense of critical reception.

To what I would hope is no one's surprise, usually these nominees are well received!

IMDb scores form a pretty close approximation of a bell curve, with most of these films hitting just around the 7.5 mark or so. While a few films fall below 7.0, you'll find the vast majority in that 7-8 range, with the mean score being 7.5/10. These folks like the nominees, sometimes love them, sometimes don't.

Both critic reception markers skew heavily towards positive reception (unsurprisingly), with an average RT critic rating of 7.8/10 and an average Metascore of 76/100, both essentially indicating strongly positive reviews bordering on unequivocal acclaim. On both ends, you see peaks around the 7.5/10 mark and the 8.5/10 mark, indicating that some of these films are solidly positive, while others really enter the unadulterated acclaim level.

In case you're wondering, that little blip on the far left of every chart is *The Boss Baby*, an unequivocal statistical anomaly on every level when it comes to reception. How that movie got nominated over *The LEGO Batman Movie* simply cannot be explained via reception alone. There was something in the water in 2017.

What conclusion can we draw from this? The obvious one: if you're an animated film and you want to get into the Oscars, people should like you! Not just a soft appreciation, but a clear and distinct level of acclaim. 3.8s on Letterboxd and 80s on Metacritic aren't the easiest to come by, but they're often what our animated contenders are picking up on the road to Oscar glory.

<h2>Who made the nominees?</h2>

The animation industry definitely has names, and, like any other branch of the Academy, the Animated Feature nominees tend to be repeat players across the tapestry of the last few years. 56% of our nominees were prior Oscar nominees to some extent, with most having been nominated or even awarded in either the Animated Feature or Animated Short category. Every once in a while, you even get an industry titan like Wes Anderson or Guillermo del Toro popping their head into the realm of animation, and the Oscars leap to shout them out.

However, newcomers should not be slept on! Every year represented in this dataset had at least two nominees who had never been nominated before. It would actually be highly anomalous to see a year full of nothing but old lags in the modern era. The Oscars are looking to reward new faces all the time, and you never know where they might pop up from.

In terms of distribution, there are a few key regulars that you see show up again and again, as certain animation studios consistently put efforts into the awards race.

The most obvious thing everyone reading this article likely already knows is that Disney is the historic juggernaut of the medium. Disney has been nominated every year of the dataset, with multiple years of getting two or even three movies in. Splitting into the different production companies of Pixar and Walt Disney Animation Studios also tells an interesting story. 10 of the 12 Pixar films released during the examined timeframe got nominated, with only *Cars 3* and *Lightyear* missing. However, only 4 of the 8 Walt Disney Animation Studios films were able to make the five in this timeframe, with *Frozen 2*, *Strange World*, *Wish*, and *Moana 2* all whiffing. While many would claim that Pixar is long past their prime, it's clear that the Oscars still have love for them, barring a significantly lukewarm reception.

If you want a consistent studio that isn't Disney, look no further than Netflix. The streaming titan has gotten at least one movie into the category every year since 2019, when its animation studio opened. Three of those seven years featured multiple nominees for them. Neon is also no slouch in this department, being represented in three of the past five years; if they pick up a buzzy indie animation title, you can count on them giving it a hefty push. GKIDS is quietly a distributor that will get standout international films into the lineup as long as they have something notable; they've gotten a nomination in five of the past nine years. Other animation studios like DreamWorks (through Universal) and Sony Pictures Animation (typically through Sony, although *KPop Demon Hunters* was a Netflix-distributed title) are significantly less consistent, but they occasionally get in with the right projects.

<h2>How are the nominees made?</h2>

There isn't much to report on this section, but I did run a couple of stats out of curiosity, so I thought I'd share the results. 60% of nominees were primarily in a 3D-animated style, 24% in 2D, and 16% in stop-motion. Clearly, the majority of nominees are in the 3D style, likely because it is currently the dominant form of animation in the Western cinematic landscape. Evidently, the Oscars are still willing to branch out and appreciate other forms of animation should they prove themselves worthy.

Additionally, while the vast majority of nominees are in English, there's still a minority of films making it in from across the globe. 20% of the nominees did not primarily include English-speaking dialogue, including three with no language, three with French, two with Japanese, and one with Danish and Russian. The Oscars still have a ways to go when it comes to considering the breadth of international animated cinema, but steps are definitely being made.

<h2>What does this mean for the 99th Oscars?</h2>

There are a few key lessons we can derive from this retrospective analysis that I think are well worth considering for the coming year as we analyze a competitive category.

**Look for films that aren't just liked, but loved.**

Early on in the year, some were looking at the new Sony Pictures Animation title *GOAT* as a possible contender. With only 3% now predicting it, the majority have obviously moved away from this, and when you look at the reception, you can see why. It had a surprisingly strong box office run, but a 3.36 on Letterboxd and a 6.4/10 average on Rotten Tomatoes simply isn't enough to sustain the necessary buzz for the Oscars.

In contrast, let's glance at the new release [*Toy Story 5*](film.html?slug=toy-story-5). As of this writing, it has a 3.79 on Letterboxd, 7.7 on IMDb, 7.8/10 on Rotten Tomatoes, and 73 on Metacritic. All of these values are in line with the average nominee for the category. This is a movie with passion and love; even if it isn't as high as the other films in the franchise, it's good enough where there's no value in betting against the nomination.

**Value branch favorites, but also don't let that keep you from predicting a buzzy new voice.**

Of the current top ten on Awards Expert, five are directed by prior Oscar-nominated filmmakers. These are *Wildwood* (Travis Knight), *Toy Story 5* (Andrew Stanton), *Forgotten Island* (Joel Crawford), *Ray Gunn* (Brad Bird), and *Julián* (Louise Bagnall). Perhaps not by coincidence, the top 5 on AE have an 80% overlap with this list, with the only difference being that *Hoppers* is in the top echelon instead of *Julián*. As a reminder, this many former nominees in the final lineup would be more than any other year since 2017. It's worth considering some of the new voices in animation that could bubble up to the top, whether it be indie voices from Cannes like Leah Nelson (*Tangles*) or Louis Clichy (*Iron Boy*), or within-studio promotions that are allowing fresh faces to guide projects from familiar studios like Steve Cox & Matthew Walker (*Shaun the Sheep: The Beast of Mossy Bottom*) or Jason Hand & Fawn Veerasunthorn (*Hexed*).

**Don't blindly follow the siren song of studios, but always keep in mind what the big ones will be doing.**

Oscar prognosticators who remember the Searchlight Meltdown of 2025 will tell you that distributors are not king, and that anything can miss or make any lineup regardless of distribution at the end of the day. I don't disagree. However, early in the season, before many films have been released, I maintain that it's highly valuable to look at what the regulars in the category have coming out and utilize that information to make more educated decisions. The distributors that get in consistently are doing that for a reason.

*Wildwood* presents an interesting contradiction of consistency between production and distribution. LAIKA has made five films (six if we include their contract work on *Corpse Bride*), and all six have been Academy Award nominated. However, the film will be distributed by Fathom, a studio that has never distributed a new release to a single Oscar nomination in any category. LAIKA films have historically been distributed by Focus Features, an Oscar mainstay, but their most recent film (*Missing Link*) still made it into the category despite the much weaker distributor, Annapurna. However, Annapurna had shown success in other Oscar categories before with surefire contenders like *Vice* and *If Beale Street Could Talk*. *Wildwood* looks to be LAIKA's most ambitious work yet, and anticipation is high; should it deliver, it may be undeniable, but one wonders whether the film could truly sustain enough awareness to take off with audiences, given such an odd distributor at the helm.

A couple of potential heavy hitters, such as *Tangles* and *Julián*, are still awaiting acquisition. Perhaps NEON will pick up one of them and launch a strong campaign aimed at more highbrow audiences. *Iron Boy* is also stuck with an odd distributor in Sony Pictures Classics, which has failed to garner any buzz in the Animated category specifically over the past couple of years. Non-starters like *Scarlet*, *A Magnificent Life*, and *The Peasants* may scare people away from risking this prediction, although *Iron Boy* received quite positive reactions out of the Cannes Film Festival, where it made its world premiere.

Overall, your predictions are your oyster. However, history is there so we might learn from it and discover new information. I hope that this article has reinforced ideas you may have already known with clear, statistical backing, and thus better informed your predictions for what looks to be an exciting category this year.
