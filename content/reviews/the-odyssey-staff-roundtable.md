This past weekend, audiences flocked to the cinema to see Christopher Nolan's *The Odyssey*. The Oscar winner's newest film debuted with an impressive $123 million domestic opening and a $263 million worldwide opening. Clearly, Nolan's epic resonated with audiences around the globe and has sparked a conversation in the zeitgeist.

To continue that conversation here on the Fantasy Filmball platform, we asked our writing staff, all of whom saw the film during opening weekend, a range of questions to see where consensus lies and where we couldn't be further apart.

The majority of our staff were among the lucky group who had purchased their tickets over a year ago for the IMAX 70mm release, and those who were not were also able to snag tickets when they went on sale a few months ago. All seeing the film the way Nolan intended us to, some even seeing the film multiple times.

<h2>First Impressions</h2>

Across the Filmball staff's reactions coming out of the theater for *The Odyssey*, one word encapsulates the group's mentality better than all, mesmerized. Whether by the epic scale of Nolan's filmmaking, the emotional weight of Odysseus's journey home, or the technical spectacle unfolding on the screen, nearly every writer described walking out of that theater feeling they were on a cinematic high.

Ray Siddiqi perhaps summed up that feeling best, writing, "A complete and unequivocal feeling of being overwhelmed. I was physically shaking... I have never been so absolutely lost in such a grippingly euphoric cinematic experience." Sean Malloy echoed that enthusiasm simply stating, "I was blown away. Nolan did it again!"

That excitement carried over across multiple viewings for some of our staff. Brad Derfner described *The Odyssey* as "this is what you get when the grandest epic in human history is adapted by the most accomplished technical director of his time," while Dylan McKercher praised how "each scene somehow gets more immersive, more memorable, and more impressive than the last, crescendoing into one of the best sequences I've ever seen put to film."

That doesn't mean the response was unanimous. Several Filmball staff members admired Nolan's ambition while acknowledging some of his writing shortcomings. Matt Downs called it "a fantastic slightly missed opportunity," citing narrative compromises, while admitting that despite "breathtaking direction and a scope of filmmaking that puts 90% of other movies to shame," he still left the theater disappointed.

Even among a range of opinions, the one thing that remained consistent was the fact that *The Odyssey* was a captivating experience. Andy Funke stated, "everything came together to make a very captivating experience, even if I did have issues along the way." Which just goes to show that even if you may have small and minor issues with the film at large, it's the type of movie you want to see multiple times on the biggest screen possible.

<h2>Who Stole the Show?</h2>

When discussing among the Filmball staff, one thing became very apparent: there was very little consensus on who the acting MVP of *The Odyssey* was. Matt Damon serves as the vessel to tell this epic tale, while Anne Hathaway had been one of the most talked-about performances even before the review embargo lifted. Those were the names most audiences expected to dominate the conversation heading into Nolan's latest spectacle, and both have firmly planted themselves in the awards conversation.

However, *The Odyssey* boasts one of the deepest ensembles Nolan has ever assembled. From the very top of the call sheet to the smallest supporting roles, nearly every actor is given a moment to leave a lasting impression. Our staff responses reflected exactly that, with almost every writer naming a different performer as their standout.

Once the review embargo lifted, Samantha Morton's Circe quickly emerged as one of the film's most celebrated scene-stealers. Despite appearing for less than ten minutes, Morton delivers what Vitor C. called a performance that "absolutely steals the show," adding that "any hype you've heard about her is simply and utterly 100% true." Andy echoed that sentiment, praising Morton as "the most well-rounded small role from both a writing and acting perspective," noting that her sequence lingered with him more than almost any other performance in the film.

John Leguizamo also drew significant support for his portrayal of Eumaeus. Terence Peterson admired the way Leguizamo "plays Eumaeus with a degree of humility and meekness that allows for his character to grow with you," while Brad simply named him as his MVP. Dylan also singled him out, arguing that his dramatic turn showcases an emotional depth audiences rarely get to see from the veteran actor.

Elliot Page proved to be another standout among the supporting ensemble. Ray praised Page's ability to "make a lot work with a little," pointing to their "soulful eyes, sneer of emotional agony, and rattling voice of anguish" that elevated every scene they appeared in. Dylan similarly highlighted Page's emotionally charged monologue during the Hades sequence, calling it "the kind of performance that reminds you why great actors deserve the opportunity to surprise audiences."

As expected, Damon and Hathaway still received plenty of love. Sean ultimately gave the edge to Damon after admitting the decision was "very difficult," while Matt jokingly answered, "Anne Hathawill and Anne Hathaway," perfectly capturing just how impressed he was with Hathaway's performance.

All of this speaks to the remarkable depth of Nolan's ensemble. While our staff couldn't agree on a single acting MVP, that's arguably one of the film's greatest strengths. Whether your standout is Damon, Hathaway, Morton, Leguizamo, Page, or another member of the cast like Tom Holland, Himesh Patel, Robert Pattinson, or Zendaya, *The Odyssey* gives nearly every performer a moment worthy of being someone's favorite. That's the hallmark of a truly exceptional ensemble.

<h2>Favorite Scenes</h2>

One reason *The Odyssey* continues to resonate across generations is because Homer's original epic has become one of the most influential stories in human history. Even if you have not read any translation of Homer's tale, you surely have seen some sort of recreation or interpretation of the saga, whether that's in *O Brother, Where Art Thou?*, *The SpongeBob SquarePants Movie*, or even the much-discussed Brad Pitt vehicle, *Troy*.

One thing that makes Homer's original so iconic is how so many of the vignettes and sequences throughout his story have been recreated over the decades. So many different people could watch this and pick out their own unique favorite sequence, and that is exactly reflected across our writers. And unlike the two previous categories, across the eight members of our staff, the stop in Hades was the clear consensus among the group.

However, multiple other scenes received multiple mentions. Vitor praised the sequence for forcing Odysseus to confront "everything that has brought him and all those who have followed him to where they are," with each revelation becoming "more and more haunting to watch." Sean also settled on Hades after admitting that his answer would be different upon every rewatch, while Brad simply named it as his favorite sequence.

On the other hand, Dylan couldn't look past the film's "10 Years of Rage" sequence, calling it "the single greatest scene of Christopher Nolan's career." He praised the way every creative department came together while arguing that Damon's monologue produced "the most emotionally affecting moment Nolan has ever put on screen." Adding that upon every rewatch, this sequence continues to recontextualize everything that comes before it to further layer this rich text. Matt joined the praise, adding that he has yet to stop thinking about Odysseus reflecting on Troy before the reveal of Athena.

Others gravitated toward the film's mythological spectacle. Terence highlighted the Sirens encounter, praising Nolan's decision to muffle the audio while allowing Odysseus' narration to fill in the emotional gaps, creating an experience that was both immersive and deeply unsettling. Andy singled out both the Cyclops and Circe sequences as the moments that completely drew him into the film.

Ray looked toward the finale, describing Penelope's challenge and its aftermath as "the greatest action set piece Nolan has ever designed," applauding the sequence's tension, emotion, and Ludwig Göransson's thunderous score.

More than anything, I think our staff replies just reinforce the age-old narrative of how iconic and important a text *The Odyssey* is, because it delivers so many memorable moments, whether it's through characters that have been expanded upon in various retellings, such as the Sirens and the Cyclops; set pieces that are instantly iconic, such as the Trojan Horse and the Land of the Dead; or, specifically for Christopher Nolan's adaptation, brand-new sequences that recontextualize the entire text, such as the "10 Years of Rage" set piece. Nolan has crafted an epic where almost every chapter and vignette of Odysseus' journey could reasonably be someone's favorite.

<h2>Crafts</h2>

When you think of Christopher Nolan and his filmography, one of the first things that comes to mind is how impressive his films look on screen. Whether that is the dedication to realism in his filmmaking style, the craftsmanship that Nolan himself and his entire behind-the-scenes department put on full display, or how grand and immersive his movies feel, *The Odyssey* exemplifies all of it in this department. While opinions differed on which craft deserves the throne, three departments were consistently mentioned above the rest: Hoyte van Hoytema's breathtaking cinematography and the film's immersive soundscape, thanks to sound designer Richard King and composer Ludwig Göransson.

Van Hoytema's work emerged as the overwhelming favorite among our writers. Vitor called the Oscar-winning cinematographer "the breathtaking champion," writing that "every shot is stunning... nothing comes short of perfection." Ray echoed that sentiment, describing the photography as "simply stupendous" and adding that experiencing *The Odyssey* in IMAX 70mm was "one of the greatest cinematic experiences of my life." Terence also praised the film's visual scope, calling Nolan's latest another reminder of just how ambitious his cinematic eye has become.

Just behind the cinematography came the film's remarkable sound design and Göransson's score. Sean admitted he'd "cheat" by choosing both together, arguing that neither would be nearly as effective without the other. Andy similarly struggled to isolate one element, but ultimately landed on Göransson's score as the aspect most likely to stay with him long after the credits rolled.

No response championed the film's sound more passionately than Dylan's. Calling it "*The Odyssey*'s greatest technical achievement," he argued that Nolan's use of overwhelming walls of sound and haunting silence creates "a masterclass in using sound not just to accompany the image, but to shape the emotional experience itself." Terence also singled out Göransson's score, praising the composer's restrained use of woodwinds and percussion, which elevated the film without ever overpowering it.

Elsewhere, Matt highlighted the production design for bringing Homer's world to life, while Brad pointed to the sheer scale of Nolan's filmmaking as the movie's most impressive accomplishment.

Whether it was the cinematography, sound, score, sets, or simply the overwhelming scale of the filmmaking itself, our staff largely agreed that *The Odyssey* represents Christopher Nolan operating at the absolute peak of his cinematic powers, truly crafting an epic worthy of the gods.

<h2>What Didn't Work</h2>

Now for the section that I'm sure most people are interested in reading, the opinions of our staff about what did not work about Nolan's *The Odyssey*. Throughout our conversations, one criticism surfaced more than any other: the film's editing. Some writers felt *The Odyssey* moved too quickly, sacrificing more intimate character moments to fit Nolan's sprawling adaptation within the three-hour runtime required by the 70mm format.

Matt perhaps summed up that sentiment best, calling the film "a four-hour masterpiece crammed into a three-hour box," arguing that "every single scene feels like a trailer for a longer version of the same scene." Vitor echoed that feeling, writing that the story "comes just shy of perfection" and would have benefited from a little more breathing room if the technical limitations of IMAX 70mm had allowed it.

Others took issue less with the pacing and more with the characters. Andy wished Penelope received additional development, while Terence argued that Nolan's supporting cast remains underwritten unless directly tied to Odysseus' journey. He also hoped the action embraced more sweeping long takes rather than rapid-fire editing, comparing the ideal approach to classics like *Lawrence of Arabia* and *Ben-Hur*.

Even Nolan's long-discussed approach to sound found its way into the conversation. Terence suggested that the pursuit of realism occasionally came at the expense of clarity, with important dialogue sometimes getting lost beneath the film's powerful sound design.

At the same time, several members of our staff simply couldn't find anything they wanted to change. Sean's answer was a blunt "Nothing," while Ray admitted that although no film is truly perfect, he "simply didn't care" about searching for flaws, believing the film's imperfections only added to its identity.

<h2>The Nolan Ranking</h2>

So where does *The Odyssey* land in the context of Nolan's career? Sean and Ray both have it at the very top. Four of us — Dylan, Terence, Brad, and Vitor — place it second, behind *Tenet* and *Interstellar* respectively. Andy slots it fifth, and Matt, our most measured voice on the film throughout, has it ninth.

<h2>Conclusion</h2>

After reflecting with the entire Filmball staff, our biggest takeaway is not that everyone agrees that *The Odyssey* is a decade-defying masterpiece, what performances stand out above the rest, or even which craft deserves the most praise. It's the fact that Nolan created yet another epic inside of his illustrious filmography that has struck the zeitgeist and spurred conversation unlike any film we have seen released so far this year.

This inspires very different reactions and opens up the conversation for what Nolan does next and what the legacy of *The Odyssey* will be. Will this be yet another award juggernaut for Christopher Nolan, leading to another Academy Awards sweep off the back of *Oppenheimer* just a few years ago? Or will this be a film that people look back on in 20 years and ask, "How did that not reap the benefits of the gods and have a one-of-a-kind Academy Award appearance?"
