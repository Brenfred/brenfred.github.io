Christopher Nolan’s newest epic might be his grandest spectacle yet. *The Odyssey* does the unthinkable: each sequence somehow becomes more immersive, more memorable, and more impressive than the last, crescendoing into one of the greatest climactic set pieces I have ever seen put to film.

One of the central ideas in both Homer's epic and Nolan's adaptation is Zeus's law, the moral code that governs Odysseus' journey home. Fittingly, I'm going to break an unwritten law of film criticism: restraint. I'm here to shamelessly praise *The Odyssey*, because Christopher Nolan's latest has earned every bit of it.

It’s no secret Nolan is one of, if not my favorite, filmmakers of all time. Throughout his career, one of Nolan's greatest strengths has always been his versatility. Whether it's the grounded realism of *Dunkirk*, Heath Ledger’s tour-de-force acting in *The Dark Knight*, the mind-bending spectacle of *Inception*, the operatic scale of *Oppenheimer*, or the out-of-body theatrical experience of *Tenet*, every film scratches a different cinematic itch. With *The Odyssey*, Nolan combines all of those strengths into one extraordinary experience. 

From the opening commandments of Travis Scott's Bard to the closing moments of this Homeric tale, *The Odyssey* delivers everything you could want. I adore the way Nolan’s adaptation of Homer’s iconic work changes the narrative of Odysseus’ grand journey. While we still hit all of our iconic beats, each stepping stone of his return to Ithaca feels like its own vignette. Each one of these scenes has its own unique feel, visual style, and cinematic identity. These are crafted and elevated due to Nolan’s command of the senses. The strongest of which, featured throughout *The Odyssey*, has to be the abundance and impact of sound. Sometimes it’s the overwhelming assault of noise during the raid on Troy. Sometimes it’s the complete absence of it, as Odysseus must prove his strength while passing the Sirens. Other times, Nolan layers diegetic and non-diegetic sounds together, letting them clash during the Trojan Horse sequence to create one of the most overwhelming sensory experiences of his career. These scenes are pushed to their absolute limit by the collaborative effort of editor Jennifer Lame, sound designer Richard King, and the heartbeat of this story, Ludwig Göransson's score. Remove any of these elements, and *The Odyssey* simply isn't the same movie.

Spectacle alone isn’t enough; it wouldn’t matter if the story didn’t work. Most of us have read *The Iliad* or *The Odyssey* at some point in school, so the broad strokes of this story aren't exactly unfamiliar. Yet Nolan's trademark nonlinear storytelling somehow restores a sense of discovery. Whether Odysseus is plotting his escape from the Cyclops, recounting his impossible journey, or reuniting with Penelope after years apart, Nolan finds new emotional weight inside one of history's oldest stories.

You could have a perfect story, you could have a master in the director’s chair, but if your actors don’t come to play, then it doesn’t work on screen. There’s been so much conversation around the casting choices of *The Odyssey*, including plenty of skepticism surrounding the casting. After seeing the film, it's difficult to imagine many of these roles being played by anyone else. Saying Matt Damon isn’t cut out to be Odysseus? Maybe I can understand that in the early parts of this epic journey, but by the time we return to Ithaca, Damon delivers one of his career-best performances. The compassion, the stoicism, the bravado, and the movie-star charisma that Damon possesses are exactly what we need in an iconic portrayal of Odysseus. Anne Hathaway? Come on. What is left to say? Can she do anything wrong? She delivers yet another show-stopping performance, bolstering her impressive filmography. She’s already having an amazing year, and *The Odyssey* is just the cherry on top. Her take as Penelope comes with so much fiery energy, and when she finally gets the opportunity to explode, you feel Penelope’s wrath.

Elliot Page also silences any lingering skepticism with a commanding performance as Cinon. Their work anchors the Land of the Dead sequence, one of the film's emotional and visual high points, and their screen presence elevates what is already among Nolan's finest set pieces. Lupita Nyong'o, meanwhile, has less screen time than some might expect, but her dual performance offers a fresh interpretation of Helen of Troy.

But I wanna shout out my MVP of *The Odyssey*: John Leguizamo as Eumaeus. As one of Odysseus’ faithful servants, Leguizamo provides the soul of the story. The whole thematic backbone of Zeus’s law is fully examined and displayed through Leguizamo’s character. He adds humor, compassion, and, most importantly, a sense of humanity to The Odyssey.

I’m not done hyping up this cast, because look at the call sheet. *The Odyssey* has one of the most stacked casts of all time. All of this has been without mentioning Robert Pattinson’s snarling villain Antinous pining over Penelope’s hand in marriage, Jon Bernthal’s macho Menelaus, Himesh Patel reuniting with Nolan after *Tenet,* giving a very nuanced performance as Eurylochus, Samantha Morton turning in a commanding take as Circe, Tom Holland showcasing that he actually has acting chops as Telemachus, and of course, Zendaya as Athena. She barely needs to speak and can tell us the world with just her eyes.

So we have great action, a director at the top of his game, a gripping screenplay, and an acting ensemble firing on all cylinders… But guess what else *The Odyssey* brings to the table? An immersive world that doesn’t feel like you’re just watching a fable unfold, but living inside of it. Hoyte van Hoytema's cinematography and Ruth De Jong's production design immerse you completely in Nolan's mythic world. Whether we're inside the Cyclops' cave, hidden within the Trojan Horse, or sailing across Poseidon's wrathful seas, the mix of cinematography, production design, and scope makes this mythical world feel grounded. Homer’s mythology becomes a reality, no longer an imagination, but given a tangible identity.

Looking back at the notes I scribbled during the screening, I realized almost every line was dedicated to a different sequence. The Cyclops. The Trojan Horse. The Sirens. The Land of the Dead. Each somehow surpassed the one before it until Nolan arrived at a back to back magnum opus level finale that rivals the Trinity test in *Oppenheimer* as the greatest sequence of his career.

When Odysseus shouts "Bring it all!" and Ludwig Göransson's pulse-pounding score erupts, *The Odyssey* reaches the kind of cinematic high that reminds you why movies are meant to be experienced on the biggest screen possible. *The Odyssey* proves it is worthy of all the hype. It’s thrilling, overwhelming, and an unforgettable journey. Christopher Nolan has once again defied the cinematic gods and delivered another film that will be discussed for generations.
