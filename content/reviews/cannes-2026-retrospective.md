It's been roughly a month since the most prestigious festival in international cinema wrapped, and according to Director Thierry Frémaux, this year's "In Competition" selection was focused more on highlighting the auteur merits of international cinema. This inclination to explore beyond the bounds of baseline Hollywood endeavors has bled into the awards space, injecting new energy into what we might consider typical Western awards tendencies. The Cannes Film Festival has historically been the main platform for international cinema and cinephiles worldwide, and, post-COVID, has become a launchpad for consideration in the Academy Awards race.

While this is beneficial for highlighting the long-term efforts of international cinema, discourse around the festival can inadvertently become bogged down in discussions of awards and prizes. Today, I want to highlight a few gems, mostly in the sidebar sections, that friends and contributors of the Fantasy Filmball team have seen. Each person was given a prompt to write about their favorite titles that any curious cinephile would be rewarded for seeing, focusing more on which films would benefit most from that word of mouth and grassroots passion. While it is generally exciting to anticipate the pulpy names and "In Competition" winners, the sidebars and other parallel sections making up the 79th Cannes Film Festival have produced some hotly anticipated flicks. Today, eight diverse films curated by the channel offer points of interest for cinephiles willing to seek them out.

<h2>Arnaud Weissenburger — <em>I'll Be Gone in June</em> &amp; <em>La Gradiva</em></h2>

An excerpt written by frequent contributor to Fantasy Filmball and international cinema connoisseur Arnaud Weissenburger. His selections of *I'll Be Gone in June* and *La Gradiva*, two feature debuts, focus on the coming-of-age period in European youth, and he emphasizes how these films converse and the resonance they hold beyond their individual stories.

**Two debuts about European youth: *I'll Be Gone in June* and *La Gradiva***

In a festival where many major auteurs failed to impress or innovate beyond their expected capacities, the films that pushed the boundaries and took risks came from a new generation of filmmakers. I could mention the vital and direct shooting style of *A Man of His Time*, which looks as though it was shot by a modern politician's social media team following them around at every corner, or the transfixing play on life, death, and rebirth in *Everytime* as expressed through bold framing and Minecraft imagery. But perhaps what impressed me most this year was seeing two debut films take on the "coming-of-age" film — a genre that has been notoriously stretched thin, as Netflix Originals these days proudly showcase — and present new directions for the genre.

It becomes pretty clear early into *I'll Be Gone in June* that it isn't content with solely recounting a year in the life of a German exchange student in New Mexico. Instead, we're dealing with a film about the malaise of a foreign teenager witnessing America's reckoning in reaction to 9/11. New Mexican desert inhabitants, being far removed from any major political or economic poles of America, are justifiably petrified to see an attack on their soil coming from countries they couldn't place on a map. But German exchange student Franny (the captivating Naomi Cosma) finds herself in the tricky spot of having to tone down her foreign characteristics, try pursuing fleeting young love, and find whatever magic remains in the experience of growing up in the land of dreams.

Dreams haunt the French students at the heart of *La Gradiva*, who go on a final class trip to Naples with their Latin teacher, anxiously awaiting the results of their college entrance exams. What director Marine Atlan explores in the high school setting is the creation and disintegration of facades: a teacher (the radiant Antonia Buresi) must lead and maintain control over her students, a class clown (the festival's revelation, Colas Quignard) must demonstrate carelessness towards lessons, the introverted bookworm (the effortlessly touching Suzanne Gerin) must let the other girls hook up with boys, and so on. Whether it's through the mysticism of Pompeii's deep history or through the natural conversations that spark on a formative trip, these facades will be tested up until their ultimatum: disappear or fall into despair.

A universal part of growing into adulthood is realizing that everyone around you, young and old, isn't so different from one another. What makes these films such strong entries as art-house "coming-of-age" stories is their understanding of the sadness of teenage disillusionment. Franny sees a charming America grow harsher and harsher, one that refuses to let her stay past the end of her school year in June (hence the title). Meanwhile, the characters' roles in *La Gradiva* completely disappear, leaving behind a bunch of equally lost adults without any greater power to guide them. These two films, and especially the extraordinary *La Gradiva*, demonstrate the challenge of seeing another person beyond their origin or function. These are attentive films that successfully reconstitute pivotal epiphanies at the tail end of childhood.

[*I'll Be Gone in June*](https://boxd.it/Tkgi) debuted in this year's Un Certain Regard section, and [*La Gradiva*](https://boxd.it/HGtE) debuted in the festival's Director's Fortnight parallel section. To keep up with what Arnaud is watching, you can follow his [Letterboxd](https://boxd.it/8nRT).

<h2>Alessio Vinciguerra — <em>Everytime</em></h2>

An excerpt from a friend of the channel, Alessio Vinciguerra, endorsing the Un Certain Regard prize-winning film [*Everytime*](https://boxd.it/11Jts) by Sandra Wollner — lifted from his published article for *Cinema Austriaco*, an online film magazine based in Austria and Italy.

*Everytime*, her third directorial work, deals with loss — one of the most universal and overexploited themes in cinema — yet without abandoning the supernatural elements that have characterised her filmography to date. Mourning, experienced through such a radical, conscious and, above all, ideologically personal perspective, will prove to be an invigorating experience, both spiritually and formally, for all viewers curious enough to follow this project.

Just as she is about to set off for Tenerife for the summer holidays, along with her mother Ella (Birgit Minichmayr) and her little sister Melli (Lotte Shirin Keiling), Jessie (Carla Hüttermann) sneaks out to enjoy one last party with her boyfriend, Lux (Tristan López). Jessie is a girl with an intense personality, whose vitality is infectious, and the camera keeps up with her until it gets lost in the strobe lights of the nightclub where they go to burn off their last reserves of energy, not forgetting to take a few pills. This time, the effects of the drugs are slow to kick in, and the couple only feel their "benefits" when they climb onto the roof of a skyscraper to watch the sunrise from a privileged perspective.

Even when reading the briefest synopsis, it's easy to connect the points and get a rough idea of what the future holds for these characters. However, it is equally impossible to be prepared for the inevitable, as Sandra Wollner intends to leave us breathless with her directorial choices, which are both unsettling and masterful. A small clue: the sun will be the only witness. Rarely in the history of cinema has such a drastic shift in tone — so violent as to cause a cinematic whiplash — been executed with such grace, at the climax of a single-take tracking shot, warmed by the gentle rays of the morning sun. Heart-wrenching, nostalgic, surprising: all key words that characterise the experience of *Everytime* from beginning to end.

<h2>Pierre-Alexandre Barillier — <em>Red Rocks</em> &amp; <em>Her Private Hell</em></h2>

Making his debut on the Fantasy Filmball channel this year (2026) is Pierre-Alexandre Barillier of [SuperSeven](https://superseven.fr/equipes), a French-based cinema criticism outlet where he serves as Web Editor. Also known as "PAB" or "PA" in our community, he's featured in this year's Cannes Film Festival video series, in which Matt and Arnaud detailed their experiences with this year's festival through vlog-style entries, along with contributing to the [Parallel Cannes series and pre-festival deliberations](https://youtu.be/WnYkK5Lxvy0?si=7-wa-BhpoSYnVVuz).

[*Red Rocks*](https://boxd.it/Mfrw) is an unusual feat for director Bruno Dumont. Beautifully shot with wide-angle lenses, handheld camera, and drone footage of nature, the movie follows a group of kids, with two falling in love à la Romeo and Juliet while another gets jealous. Don't really expect any more plot from that, because this is still a Bruno Dumont movie — *Red Rocks* is purely a slice-of-life movie following kids whose only hobby is jumping from, well, big red rocks into the sea while driving around on mini-quads. A movie that balances the bizarre — the shots are always very long, focusing on the kids' faces, prompting the actors to break the camera's gaze, wondering why it is that long — and the tender — childhood has never been shown on screen with such truth and beauty of the moment. This is not a movie for everybody; it'll demand your full attention — even though it's only 90 minutes! — but for those ready to be immersed in that Côte d'Azur summer with hilarious child actors, this is a must-see from the Quinzaine des Cinéastes.

One of the most hated movies of this year's Cannes, *Her Private Hell* finds Nicolas Winding Refn at his most radical. The movie is betting on whether you're willing to go with it on a bat-shit atmospheric piece almost devoid of any plot point. What you follow is mood pieces, genre homages, and beautiful actors — we know Refn loves that! — punctuated by an all-timer score from master Pino Donaggio, whose music will infuse romanticism into the macabre, (pink) light into the mist that surrounded most of the audience in Cannes, but that I've rightfully entered to live a true spectacle of mise-en-scène.

<h2>Zinc — Three from the Croisette</h2>

Three contributions from Zinc (aka [detective Zinc](https://boxd.it/3tBdd)), another friend of the channel — a Chinese film critic based in Germany and a frequent attendee of the Cannes and Venice Film Festivals.

***The Unknown / L'inconnue*** (Arthur Harari) — In Competition: An all-time favorite. Harari turns the mystery between men and women into consummate suspense, where desire moves from the body into the depths of consciousness, opening a journey of intimacy, identity, and self-awareness.

***Words of Love / Quelques mots d'amour*** (Rudi Rosenberg) — Un Certain Regard: A surge of warmth and heartbreak pouring through the ruins and repairs of family bonds, rooted deeply in everyday life, raw sincerity, and the sorrow left behind by an absent father. Hafsia Herzi is wonderful as a mother fighting with everything she has to carve out happiness for her children, and her instinctive, fiercely naturalistic performance once again proves why she reigns as one of French cinema's great naturalist leading actresses.

***Orange-Flavoured Wedding / Mariage au goût d'orange*** (Christophe Honoré) — Cannes Premiere: A grand ensemble feast of French actors, with Honoré laying bare a deeply private childhood story of birth, bloodline, family, hidden tragedy, exile, failure, and sparks of homophobic violence, all still bound together by love and kinship.

Wrapping up, I want to express my gratitude to the four contributors to this retrospective. As these four are attendees of the 79th Cannes Film Festival, I recommend that readers use the platforms above to experience a wide range of international cinema. Arnaud, Vinci, PA, and Zinc are great representatives of everything the Filmball community is — as both contributors and friends of the channel and, now, the website. There is a beauty in seeking films beyond the primary purview of one's cinematic accessibility (both formally and thematically). I hope this retrospective encourages seeking out these new and formally energetic works, which could be described as "gems" for those looking beyond the vaunted company of "In Competition" winners and the festival's venerable filmmakers.
