*Is God Is*, the debut feature film by playwright Aleshea Harris, and it follows the story of twin sisters Racine and Anaia, both covered in third degree burns that they got in an accident when they were children - an accident that they believe left them orphaned, as they were tossed around the foster care system for years afterwards. When Racine and Anaia receive a letter from their mother, the twins are thrown into a bloody quest for revenge. As her dying wish, their mom (or “God”) requests that the twins kill the man who set them on fire and left them for dead - their father.

What follows is a stylish, smart thriller which is as thought provoking as it is bloody. Harris’s film may be immensely satisfying on the level of a pure revenge thriller, as it delivers on the carnage that one might crave from a Sam Peckinpah or Quentin Tarantino film - but it has so much more on its mind than just a pile of dead bodies. *Is God Is* handles topics of feminine rage, misogynoir, and society’s dismissal of violence against Black women, and it does it all under the umbrella of a classically styled action movie.

But the most remarkable thing about *Is God Is,* is that it didn’t start as a movie. This is an adaptation of Aleshea Harris’s 2016 play of the same name, which ran Oﬀ-Broadway, winning 3 Obie Awards, for writing, direction, and the performance of both twin sisters. I have not personally seen or read the play, but in viewing the film, I’m struck by a feeling that I rarely feel when watching a stage to screen adaptation - I have no idea how this would work on stage, it just feels so perfectly fit to the cinematic medium.

There’s a bit of a curse that happens in adapting a piece of theater to the cinema. Despite the inherent similarities of the stage and the screen - both mediums feature actors reading lines, wearing costumes, with sets, props, special eﬀects, music, all within a similar timeframe of (typically) 90 minutes to 2.5 hours. However, in adaptation from the stage to the screen, something that is typically NOT factored in are the inherent diﬀerences between the mediums - editing being the biggest one (and possibly the one true feature that distinguishes the cinema from any other art form). A film can move from place to place, from time to time, in a way that the theater is incapable of doing without major suspension of disbelief. It’s for this reason that most plays are written to include as few “edits” as possible, as the strength of the theatrical medium comes from observing themes play out over lengthy dialogues. Cinema, on the other hand, finds its strength in depicting actions. So while a lengthy dialogue may be riveting to watch on stage, that same dialogue may put audiences to sleep in the cinema if it is not also linked to physical actions. This is the point where most stage to screen adaptations fail. They simply try to take the things that work on the stage and capture those exact things on film, failing to acknowledge that these two mediums work for entirely diﬀerent reasons. There’s a reason why we say that films like *August: Osage County* or *Ma Rainey’s Black Bottom* feel too “Stagey”.

This is exactly where *Is God Is* succeeds. It truly adapts to the new medium, breaking scenes of dialogue up across locations, giving characters visual actions to perform rather than just letting them talk, and adding in long sequences with no dialogue at all. Aleshea Harris has such a keen understanding of the theatrical form and the ways that certain ideas may not translate exactly to film - specifically the fourth wall breaking monologues that each character gives to tell us exactly how they’re feeling. But rather than just throwing these ideas out entirely, Harris looks through a new lens to find cinematic ways to represent these more theatrical ideas. What would have been characters directly addressing the audience on stage becomes on-screen text representing a psychic connection between twins, or stylized narration from a character who has no tongue, bouncing between the past and present through black & white flashbacks that move between voiceover narration and characters directly addressing the camera, and internal thoughts written as monologues are delivered as dialogue establishing the strange quirks and deep connection between twins instead. Suddenly, what was written for the stage feels at home on the screen, all because it’s being handled by a writer and director who understands not only how the mediums are intertwined, but how they diﬀer from one another.

Stylistically, however, the film isn’t just an invention of new ideas of how to adapt from the stage to the screen, it’s also a greatest hits reel of 50 years worth of genre cinema. While the easiest reference to pull is *Kill Bill*, I would urge viewers to look further back at some of the films that inspired that movie. As mentioned earlier, Sam Peckinpah seems to be a major reference point, with his films *Straw Dogs* and *The Wild Bunch*, and their gritty, gory treatment of violence. There’s also a lot of Sergio Leone and other Spaghetti Westerns, especially in the way it handles the topic of revenge, the pregnant pauses and knowing looks exchanged between characters, and those massive close-ups on the magnificent Kara Young, where she almost feels like Clint Eastwood chomping on a cigar. With the film’s Louisiana setting and the constant scenes taking place on the edge of the sun-baked asphalt of the highway, the Southern Gothic genre is also an important reference point. In all of these works and references, it’s very important to think historically of the role of Black bodies in these genres. In the Spaghetti Western, if Black characters were involved at all, they typically took the role of slaves or victims. Three of the most famous examples of the Southern Gothic are *In the Heat of the Night, The Defiant Ones,* and *Mississippi Burning*, all of which are films about racist violence in the south, and which primarily feature Black characters as victims, while white protagonists save the day by stopping the evil white antagonists. They are also all notably made by white filmmakers. Even *In the Heat of the Night*, which features Sidney Poitier in one of the lead roles, is still primarily seen through the lens of the white detective played by Rod Steiger (I’ll pause quickly here to mention one notable example of Southern Gothic told from a Black perspective, which is *Eve’s Bayou* by Kasi Lemmons, but still that’s just one example - albeit a VERY good one). It is only quite recently that Black filmmakers have been embraced in genre filmmaking without their films being labelled as “blaxploitation” or something “other” to mainstream genre - many Black lead southern gothic films, or westerns, or horror films of the past were marketed and sold primarily as Black film rather than being marketed and sold as southern gothic, western, or horror. The use of genre within *Is God Is* is directly responding to this, and the fact that every single actor in the film is Black is an important part as well, because even in recent Black genre fare like *Sinners* or *The Harder They Fall*, Blackness is still put into opposition with the white characters and the white world and institutions around them. *Is God Is* is an outright southern gothic spaghetti western revenge thriller, and all of the characters are Black without it having to be explained or justified through a lens of racism or “Otherness”.

But far more importantly than genre film, Aleshea Harris’s script is drawing from the classical ideas of Greek Tragedy. Of course, we can immediately point to the Oedipal storyline of murdering the father (though no mother is married in this one). We can also look to the somewhat divine framing of the story, as the quest to kill their father is given to the twins by “God”. We can also point to the way that this uses internal monologue like a Greek chorus, or the structural format of a prologue, three episodes (the church group, the lawyer, the father), and an *exodus*, or conclusion. It also follows Aristotle’s *unity of action*, which declared that a play should have one core action, with few or no subplots. It’s a classical Greek tragedy in structure and execution, except for one thing - the tragedy of it all. In a classical tragedy, the main character must have a “tragic flaw”. Something that ultimately leads to their doom, their destruction, usually by divine punishment. While doom and destruction certainly happen in *Is God Is*, it hardly feels like tragedy or divine punishment - in fact, after the opening, “God” is nowhere to be seen. While a classical Greek tragedy would try to make its point about rage as a destructive force, *Is God Is* embraces its character’s tragic flaw as something entirely human, entirely real. In one key moment of the film, Sterling K Brown’s father character brushes oﬀ the anger of his daughter by saying that the situation in which he lit his entire family on fire “had a lot of nuance”. As if his daughter has no right to be angry since she doesn’t understand all sides of the story. So often in media, stories will be told with this “both sides” approach, giving equal weight to the perpetrator and the victim. Often, it seems like violent men attempt to use the emotions of their victims to discredit them or paint them as overreacting. Feminine rage has historically been dismissed, not taken seriously, or seen as being overly emotional, while masculine rage is seen as an inevitable and justified response. But in *Is God Is*, a film written by, directed by, starring women, the film takes the rage of its characters completely seriously. It allows both Racine and Anaia to experience the intensity of these emotions without ever painting them as hysterical or overreacting. It allows for their anger to be their strength, not their downfall, not something to be punished for.

It’s also a commentary on society’s view of “ugliness”, and how true ugliness comes from within the heart of an evil person, and how acts of ugliness leave marks that are visible and called ugly on the victim, while leaving the perpetrator spotless. Racine and Anaia are both scarred and disfigured from their father setting them on fire as children - an act that has marked them forever, having other kids call them ugly. Meanwhile, their father has been able to move on and have three other children, saying that his past is just a small moment or phase for him, that he has changed. His ugly actions live on the skin of his daughters. But he just gets to live. It’s also worth noting that both of the girls are burned on their right arms, the same arms that they use to bludgeon multiple people to death. As if the pain and hatred that was passed into them is being channeled out through the same skin, the same scars. It feels almost like a prophecy that a Greek oracle would foretell - that the scars one inflicts on others will one day come back to harm them too.

In an era where nearly every revenge film seems to end with some variation of “vengeance is an endless and unproductive cycle”, having the revenge-seeking protagonist walk away and being the bigger person, it’s refreshing to have a film that is truly about vengeance served cold. There is immense value in those films about forgiveness, or about how the first step of healing is walking away and leaving the past behind. There is also immense value in a film that has no interest in healing or forgiving, and instead deals with trauma by smashing it in the face with a blunt object tucked into a tube sock. Is it the healthiest message? Not necessarily. But it’s honest, it’s raw, and it doesn’t dismiss the feelings of its characters when almost any other film would.*Is God Is* is remarkable. With a cast full of great performances, masterful direction, and a script with layers upon layers of thematic heft, you wouldn’t be blamed for doubting that the film could also be this fun. It’s a movie that does everything, balances between the intellectual and the visceral, between theory and pure entertainment. If you’re going to the movies to have a good time, *Is God Is* will do just that. If you’re deep into reading bell hooks and want something to blow your mind, *Is God Is* will do that too.
