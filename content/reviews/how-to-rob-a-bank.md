David Leitch is easily one of my favorite action directors working today. His previous two films, 2022’s *Bullet Train* and 2024’s *The Fall Guy*, are two of my all-time favorite popcorn flicks, combining charismatic lead performances, an infectiously humorous tone, and inventive action sequences, likely thanks to Leitch’s background in stunt work. So, when I learned that his newest film, *How to Rob a Bank*, would center on a group of bank robbers who use social media to their advantage, I was seated. I have always loved a good heist movie, and combining what I already adored about Leitch’s films with a premise clearly aimed at Generation Z, the film seemed tailor-made for me.

Unfortunately, *How to Rob a Bank* is an unrecognizable step down from Leitch’s prior work, completely devoid of his trademark charm. It features painfully unnatural dialogue, a thinly written cast of characters, and several vain attempts to appeal to younger audiences. Quite often, I found myself laughing out loud at the screenplay, though only sometimes for the reasons the filmmakers intended. For a director who had seemingly found his groove, this is one of the year’s more notable disappointments.

The film follows an eccentric crew, led by Ryan (Nicholas Hoult), that executes and documents meticulously planned bank robberies through over-the-top social media posts. Inspired by the high-energy content that TikTok has made so popular, the robbers gradually build a large and devoted online following. Importantly, these criminals are not interested in money for personal gain alone: At the start of the film, they already have more than enough money to retire overseas. This is a fact that veteran FBI agent West (John C. Reilly) makes sure to explain to the audience several times, just in case we somehow manage to forget it.

But the crew sees things differently. They position themselves as modern-day Robin Hoods, fighting against what they perceive as an exploitative financial system. For example, Ryan and his partner, Emi (Anna Sawai), lost their son, Ren, after they could not afford the medical bills necessary for his life-saving treatment, while Army veteran Vince (Pete Davidson) developed lung cancer during his service and felt abandoned by the system upon returning home. As the group’s online popularity surges, West enlists the reluctant help of Reagan Gardner (Zoë Kravitz), a disillusioned software engineer who agrees to assist only because West promises to get her sentence commuted.

This premise, though full of clichés, still allows Leitch room to explore themes of social media fame, economic inequality, and growing distrust of financial institutions. But writer Mark Bianculli’s screenplay offers little beyond the most basic implications of these ideas. Each robber receives a tragic backstory that could ostensibly explain their turn to criminality, but the film simply fails to build layered characters around these one-line explanations.

This failure is especially frustrating given how many characters compete for attention throughout the film’s 122-minute runtime. Ryan and Emi should, in my opinion, have been the focus of the plot, giving it an emotional foundation. Instead, their relationship is completely underdeveloped, leaving the audience with no one to root for. The dialogue only exacerbates the script’s structural issues, as Bianculli repeatedly attempts to force the humor that characterized Leitch’s previous films, delivering jokes that frequently feel AI-generated. The problem is not necessarily that the humor is juvenile, it’s not as though *Bullet Train* was highbrow art-house cinema, but rather that the film lacks the personality to make such an approach work.

Part of the problem may also lie in the absence of the magnetic performances that have anchored Leitch’s previous films. Hoult is certainly a talented actor, but he lacks the same larger-than-life presence and crowd-pleasing appeal in this particular role, and the screenplay offers him little opportunity to compensate. Without a compelling central character, Leitch’s familiar mixture of action and comedy becomes considerably harder to sustain.

Nowhere is the film’s desire to court younger audiences more apparent than in its soundtrack. The opening scene is set to Fred Again’s “Victory Lap,” loudly and immediately announcing the demographic it hopes to attract. From there, the film features a seemingly endless and increasingly obnoxious procession of needle drops, including music from Billie Eilish and Childish Gambino. One particularly jarring moment abruptly shifts from a serious exchange between Ryan and Emi into an extended make-out sequence set to Eilish’s music. The scene just felt “made for streaming,” fully encapsulating my issues with the film overall. Rather than using songs to complement the storytelling, the film, especially in its first half, resembles a playlist desperately searching for a narrative.

In the end, *How to Rob a Bank* is a highly disappointing production from a filmmaker whom I would consider myself a huge fan of. Ultimately, this effort lacks the charisma, humor, and emotional investment necessary to work, even with some solid action sequences. For a movie so concerned with cultivating an online following, *How to Rob a Bank* seems to have forgotten the importance of giving audiences something worth caring about.
