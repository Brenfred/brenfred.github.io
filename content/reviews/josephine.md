Every year, every festival, every movie, you’re searching for that one film that absolutely blows you away. The kind of movie that reminds you why you love attending festivals in the first place. Why you put yourself through the pain of watching four movies in a day, cramming your 6’4 self into theater seats where you can’t feel your knees anymore. Why you commit nearly 28 hours in the span of 4 days to watch movies instead of enjoying the fresh fall air or spending time with your girlfriend or cat. You endure all of it for the chance to discover something that completely knocks you off your feet, something that takes your breath away, something that reminds you why you fell in love with cinema in the first place. And this year at the Philadelphia Film Festival, for me, that film is *Josephine*.

*Josephine* is the most impactful, the most emotional, and the most important movie I’ve seen this fall festival season. Mason Reeves delivers a once-in-a-lifetime child acting performance that doesn’t even need the “child” modifier to justify its praise. I’m sure that by the end of 2026, I’ll still be banging the drum for Reeves, arguing that she has delivered my favorite actress performance of the year. For a seven-year-old, the amount of compassion, rage, innocence, and heart she wears on her sleeve is absolutely enthralling. Watching a child lose her innocence in real time is something no one should ever have to do & Reeves makes that experience devastatingly believable. Even when surrounded by career-best performances from Channing Tatum and Gemma Chan, all you can think about is how fantastic Reeves is. There are at least six different scenes where she completely wins you over with her abilities, reminding you why you love going to the theater… to witness breakthrough talent, discover fresh stories, & experience the emotional power that makes cinema such a special medium.

Beth de Araújo, the writer/director, has earned a new lifelong fan. I thought this was her debut, but apparently it’s not, so her previous film, *Soft & Quiet*, is immediately going on my watchlist and will be watched before the end of October. That’s the real sign of a film you absolutely adore: it makes you want to seek out everything else its creator has done. And after seeing what de Araújo accomplishes here, I can’t wait to see what she does next.

I’m in awe of how de Araújo constructs a narrative and shoots her films. There are multiple sequences throughout Josephine where I feel like I’m watching the next big thing being discovered in real time, whether it’s the actress on my screen or the woman behind the camera. There’s a dinner table scene with Tatum, Chan, a police officer, & Reeves where the camera spins in a complete circle, 360, round and round, following whoever happens to be speaking. It’s such a simple concept on paper, but watching the camera glide between these characters makes you feel like you’re right there in the room with them, absorbing every reaction and every uncomfortable pause. I remember sitting there thinking I was watching greatness unfold.

And somehow, de Araújo tops herself toward the end of the film with a courtroom sequence. In one uninterrupted take, de Araújo’s camera tracks from outside into the courtroom with our core family, shifting between whoever is speaking: we go from lawyer A, Josephine, lawyer B, back to Josephine, then to the judge, and so on. The camera continues moving seamlessly, never losing track of the conversation or the emotional weight of the moment. You’re not just watching a technically impressive shot… you’re watching a filmmaker who understands how to use the camera to pull you into a scene and keep you there on the edge of your seat, waiting for each new cross-examination and response to go deeper and deeper. It’s the kind of filmmaking that makes you sit back and appreciate the sheer craft of what you’re watching while simultaneously getting swept up in the story. Between de Araújo’s direction & Reeves’ performance, there are moments where you can practically feel a major talent announcing herself to the world.

From its opening moments, *Josephine* punches you in the face and leaves you emotionally devastated. But as you surrender yourself to de Araújo, Mason Reeves, Chan, and Tatum for the next two hours, you realize that devastation isn’t the same thing as hopelessness. There’s a tenderness in the way *Josephine* handles its characters, a sense that even when life is cruel & unfair, there’s still room for us to feel compassion, to feel connection, and for us to heal as a group. Despite the heaviness of what unfolds from the opening moments and the lasting ramifications for the following 120 minutes, I never felt like the film was abandoning me to feel that pain on my own. Instead, I felt that *Josephine* cared for me, understood what I needed at each passing moment, and reminded me of the importance of simply having someone being there for one another.

This is what film festivals are all about… discovering a film you might never come across at your local AMC or Regal on any given weekend, sitting in a packed theater with 450 other Philadelphians, & collectively experiencing something that stays with you long after the final shot. It’s about finding a film that makes the exhausting schedules, the aching knees, and the countless hours spent inside a dark theater feel completely worthwhile. *Josephine* reminded me why I do all of this in the first place. I keep coming back to these festivals year after year because I am searching for that one film that makes everything else disappear for a couple of hours. *Josephine* is a film that isn’t afraid to show that life may not always be pretty, and sometimes the stories we need to hear are the hardest ones to sit through. But sometimes, in the darkness of a theater, a film can make you feel a little less alone. Sometimes, that’s more than enough.
