In the months leading up to the 2026 Cannes Film Festival, I had heard rumors that Na Hong-jin's *Hope*, the highly anticipated follow-up to his cult classic *The Wailing*, would likely land a slot outside of the main competition. Being awarded any slot at such a prestigious festival, combined with the idea of an accomplished filmmaker taking on the challenge of a massive alien thriller, was enough to have me intrigued already. But when the final Cannes lineup arrived, *Hope* found itself inside the main competition, fighting for the famed Palme d'Or. The hype around the movie only continued to build from here, reaching a fever pitch around the film's May 17 premiere, where 23% of Awards Expert users were predicting it to take home the Palme. However, once people saw the film, the story changed. Unfortunately, after receiving mixed reviews, marked by a 66 [Metacritic](https://www.metacritic.com/movie/hope-2026/) average, *Hope* ultimately left the Cannes award ceremony empty-handed. After finally getting the chance to see the film, I can see why.

*Hope* features multiple spectacular action set pieces, an effective sense of humor and certain stretches that remind you exactly why the film generated so much excitement. However, the connective tissue between these positives is shockingly non-existent. Set in the fictional, remote South Korean village of Hope Harbor, the film follows police chief Bum-seok (Hwang Jung-min) as a series of increasingly violent attacks suggests something strange is at work. As hard as Bum-seok tries, reinforcements from other police stations are unavailable and the phone lines are quickly damaged, forcing Bum-seok to confront the threat with only the help of fellow officer Im Sung-ae (Jung Ho-yeon), local hunter Ko Sung-ki (Zo In-sung) and the rest of the villagers.

In its opening act, *Hope* places much of the larger story out of play. Na keeps the creatures out of sight for much longer than I anticipated, building suspense through the destruction they leave behind. Screams and animalistic growls echo from beyond the frame, and we are only privy to the increasingly gruesome aftermath as Bum-seok tries to figure out what to do.

Although this approach works to make these scenes suspenseful, the eventual reveal of the first alien, rife with shoddy CGI, is a bit underwhelming. Despite the anticlimactic reveal, this suspense demonstrates where *Hope* works best, and it also delivers considerably more wit than I expected. The best of the comedy comes in interactions between Bum-seok and Ahn Nak-yeon (Lee Sang-hee), an older man with whom Bum-seok develops a "buddy cop" dynamic for the first section of the movie. Hwang finds a panicked comedic pace that makes his interpretation of a police chief entertaining and hilarious as the world around him collapses, putting *Hope* at its strongest when horror, action and comedy collide.

The issue, though, is that we know nearly nothing about Bum-seok outside of this crisis, and his eventual transformation from scared cop to brave hero feels obligatory more than anything else. However, even though Bum-seok's character development is weak, he might still be the strongest in the ensemble, since none of the characters are given any meaningful backstory, relationships or personality to speak of. Sung-ae, Sung-ki and any number of other villagers are placed in danger, but the larger suspense does not come from any remote attachment to the actual people involved. Considering the film's 156-minute runtime, it's almost impressive how little time *Hope* dedicates towards its characters.

The extraterrestrials, whom Na — as with the human characters — continually tells us nothing about, start to feel more like a liability than natural suspense. However, in its final few scenes, *Hope* pulls a hard 180-degree turn, and unloads a significant amount of alien mythology in one expository dump. This conclusion attempts to explain some of the relationships and motivations for the aliens, alongside the launch of a larger story, but somehow I walked away more confused than before.

Clearly, Na has much more to tell us about these aliens. *Hope* does not make any effort to hide its sequel ambitions, closing on a cliffhanger that includes a city-sized alien ship crashing into the Korean countryside. If future films expand upon the aliens, I could easily imagine my opinion of *Hope* improving in response, but, if we never get the sequel, this third-act twist would feel incredibly underwhelming.

Perhaps we will receive another installment that provides some of the missing pieces, but for now, *Hope* exists as a stand-alone, ambitious and frequently exhilarating but messy popcorn flick. Still, there's enough audacity in Na's filmmaking that I would happily return to Hope Harbor if given the chance, but I just hope he gives me someone to care about next time.
