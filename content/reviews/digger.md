There’s no easy way to review this *Digger* because it openly defies what a “film” even is. There are no clear acts, there is no catharsis, and, based on reasons that fall into spoiler territory, not even the “protagonist” is all that clear. The one thing that *is* clear is that the film functions as a satire, not just of American politics or the climate crisis, but of the very works of art that satirize those things. A satire on satire. A satire of itself, even. And the results are fascinating.

*Dr. Strangelove*, *Asteroid City*, *Opening Night*, and the works of Roy Andersson are thrown into a blender to tell the tale of Digger Rockwell, an oil tycoon played by a prosthetically aged Tom Cruise, in his most ambitious role since *Magnolia*. After discovering that his negligence has caused an iceberg off the coast of Greenland to break apart and head toward Europe, which will inevitably cause a destructive environmental disaster, he meets with President Compson (John Goodman) and his White House staff to decide how to handle matters. Should they warn the Europeans and admit culpability? Should they nuke the berg? Or should they pretend they know nothing of the matter? Rounding out the cast are Riz Ahmed, Sandra Hüller, Michael Stuhlbarg, and Jesse Plemons.

Iñárritu has a bone to pick. With his critics, his audience, other filmmakers… with himself. In *Birdman*, he takes shots at the superhero movie and how it degrades the medium. He challenges the critics, who wield the power to ruin artistic careers while never having to bare their souls to an audience or take any risks themselves. He pokes fun at other artists, who embark on “prestige” projects as a way to satisfy their own vain desires. In *Bardo*, he turns the mirror on himself, questioning his own place as an artist, a Mexican, and a member of society.

And then we get *Digger*, which dials his critique to eleven. The film cynically lambasts American imperialism. The billionaire capitalists ruining our planet. The liberal hypocrites who benefit from their crimes. We’ve seen this all before. But where AGI takes it further is in questioning the very act of satirizing these things. What is all this good for? Is he just another Riggan Thomson, making art as a means to satisfy his vain desires? Isn’t that what all these other artists do with their pearl-clutching projects that take home a dozen awards because they make the elite feel like they’re “doing the right thing,” even though they’re all just as guilty of enabling the atrocities of our planet? Did any one of the hundreds or thousands of Oscar voters who watched and voted for *Don’t Look Up* in early 2022 do absolutely anything to practice what the film preached? Did the filmmakers? Or is it all just a way to pat each other on the back while Elon Musk continues to amass more wealth than the bottom 35% of Americans combined?

These are the questions *Digger* poses. Whether it works as a viewing experience or not is almost irrelevant.  Iñárritu isn’t seeking for his audience to have a good time. He’s making art that questions its own futility, which will inevitably make the audience question what the point of watching it even was, and the filmmaker is aware of this. This is what makes reviewing *Digger* so difficult. Not because I’d have to dance around a couple of big spoilers, but because it’s near-impossible to judge it based on my preconceived notions of what movies are. To do so would be a fool’s errand. It’d be to impose what I believe *Digger* should have been, as opposed to accepting that its ambitions are often in direct opposition to its audience’s desires.

So what? The film is immune to criticism because it is explicitly aware of its own weaknesses? Critics will obviously disagree (though, based on Riggan’s monologue about criticism in *Birdman*, I doubt Iñárritu really gives a damn). “Let the critics criticize — or decide how to even critique — this film,” AGI will say, as he continues to speak his truth while he still has the platform to do so.

As far as awards are concerned, it’s hard to see the film’s mixed response not hurting its chances in a crowded Best Picture lineup. If the Academy really hates it, I can see it blanking altogether. That said, I can also see a movie this divisive having a strong, loyal group of supporters that puts it at number one on certain ballots. Especially from the international contingent, which seems to like it more than the domestic one. And it’s worth noting that no Iñárritu film has ever not gotten an Academy Award nomination. If even just for Makeup and Hairstyling or Production Design, my guess is that *Digger* makes some sort of appearance at next year’s Oscars. Anyone who was hoping Cruise would win his first Academy Award, though, may have to wait a little longer.

Watching *Digger* in 2026 is akin to what I believe it’d have been like to see Duchamp’s *Fountain* in 1917, or Warhol’s *Campbell’s Soup Cans* in 1962. Works of art that transcend the socially imposed bounds of what art is, or is meant to accomplish. As a member of the film-watching community who has had these boundaries imposed upon me, I can’t say I loved *Digger* (in fact, I gave it three stars on my personal Letterboxd account). But I strongly believe in voting with my dollar. I want to see more movies that challenge us, rather than just ones that seek only to give us exactly what we want. If a glowing 4-star review on a public platform does anything to help make that happen, it’s a stance I’m willing to take.
