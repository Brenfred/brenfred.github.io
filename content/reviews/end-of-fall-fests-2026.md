Every year, awards season begins, but not at the same time for everyone. For some, it starts and ends at the Oscars. To others, it starts over a year in advance. But to many, it starts in September with the fall festivals. Toronto, Venice, Telluride, New York, and a good few other festivals premiere some of the season's biggest successes and flops. Here's what you need to know about the biggest moment for awards in 2026.

The big headline of films right now isn't anything in theaters, but rather the biggest crash of the season, that being Tom Cruise and Alejandro González Iñárritu's *Digger*. While it will certainly have a following behind it, the overall reactions are highly divisive. Some have compared it to *Don't Look Up*, which earned a Best Picture nomination, but *Digger* won't be helped by its dismal box office performance: its opening-day gross was half of *Obsession*'s, on a budget over 200 times the size. Considering Warner Bros.' assimilation into Paramount, *Digger* will be the studio's last film not under Paramount, and as bold of a swing as it is a way to go, it may not be the swan song David Zaslav would've hoped for.

Another flop, maybe a little less catastrophically, is that of *The Social Reckoning*. Aaron Sorkin's fourth directorial effort seems poised to be his first with zero Oscar nominations, following mixed-to-negative reviews from critics. While the few audiences who have seen it haven't quite panned it, *The Social Reckoning* is still easily Sorkin's most maligned film, and now finds itself in a difficult position.

On a more positive note, Georgia Oakley's *Sense & Sensibility* has drawn strong audience reactions and solid critic scores. While the days of it being a potential Best Picture contender seem to have passed, it may still be a strong craft contender this season. Many awards seasons have produced notable releases during the fall festivals, such as last year with *One Battle After Another*, but this year the fall festivals seem to have the full focus of awards season.

<h2>Venice</h2>

Venice is often a highlight of the season, and this year comes as no exception. The immediate highlight has to be Martin McDonagh's *Wild Horse Nine*, which, although it premiered to middling critic reactions, has steadily earned better reviews and audience acclaim. *Wild Horse Nine* seems to be in the consensus Best Picture top five, and while time may tell whether it holds, the odds are looking up for this fantastic cast.

Alongside *Wild Horse Nine*, Venice produced two other major winners, being *NAZA* and *Possible Love*. *NAZA* is the follow-up to *No Other Land*, with Rachel Szor and Yuval Abraham once again at the helm. After receiving incredible acclaim at Venice, the film seems poised to enter an awards season with an already stacked documentary slate. Even so, there's debate over whether *NAZA* is the festival's most acclaimed film, given the release of Lee Chang-dong's *Possible Love*. *Possible Love* premiered to resounding reviews, winning the Grand Jury Prize at Venice and finishing runner-up for the International People's Choice Award at Toronto.

Venice also produced interesting results for two films. First, the big winner of the Golden Lion was *Woman Unknown*. Despite the strength of many contending films, *Woman Unknown* won the award as a bit of a shock, because despite great critic scores, it received very middling reactions from audiences at Venice. A much more notable reaction, though, was the outrage over Danny Boyle's *Ink*. The film sparked controversy over its use of AI, which largely overshadowed its otherwise positive reception. The film is well liked, but in the end, it's a PR nightmare and potentially Netflix's fourth priority, making it a relatively hard film to bet on success for.

The three most notable losses at Venice include a black sheep: Lance Oppenheim's *Primetime*. While the film didn't draw widespread negative reactions, it still fell short of what many hoped for. Robert Pattinson and Skyler Gisondo have been raved about, but it doesn't quite seem like it will hold up into awards season.

Two much more egregious failures are *Bunker* and *Bucking Fastard*. Both films exited the festival with very mixed word of mouth, and while both made long runs on the festival circuit, they failed to garner much positive word of mouth. Easily the two most high-profile full-blown flops at Venice.

Telluride is often a hub for positivity, with a consistent slate of award-worthy films every year. This year's strongest debut at the festival may, quite literally, be *The Debut*. Jesse Eisenberg's film premiered to phenomenal critic and audience reactions, and while those haven't held up quite as well, the film still seems perfectly set to contend, with Julianne Moore finally challenging *Obsession*'s Inde Navarrette. *The Debut* was the easier of two wins to see coming at the festival, the other being *You Can See Everything*. The previously unknown documentary has generated massive buzz as an awards contender, potentially unlike any documentary ever. Directed by Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim, it has earned rave reactions, briefly breaking Letterboxd records and earning sky-high scores on all of Rotten Tomatoes, Metacritic, and IMDb.

The only real loss to note at Telluride may be that of Andrew Haigh's *A Long Winter*, which received extremely mixed reactions. While critics may have a brief burst of attention for the film's cinematography, it doesn't seem to have as much traction this season as Haigh's *All of Us Strangers* did that year.

The last two premieres to discuss are those of *Tender Loving Care* and *Elsinore*, both of which succeeded in entirely different ways. *Tender Loving Care* is a critical darling, boasting incredible scores. *Elsinore* is an audience darling, winning the Toronto International Film Festival's International People's Choice Award (what a mouthful). At the end of the day, both could be easy contenders if it weren't for their distribution. *Elsinore* will have to wrestle with *Obsession* for Focus's focus this awards season, while Bleecker Street's notoriously negative record may prove the downfall for Mike Leigh's supposed swan song.

The only film mentioned today to have premiered before the fall season is *La Bola Negra*. The high-profile Cannes film rocked Toronto, winning the People's Choice Award and earning widespread audience acclaim and applause. With *La Bola Negra* taking the top prize, it's no surprise the two runners-up, *Being Heumann* and *I Play Rocky*, also won at TIFF.

*Being Heumann* received mixed-to-positive reviews, but in a stunning upset, it made the top three over high-profile contenders such as *Wild Horse Nine*, *Club Kid*, and *The Debut*. *Being Heumann*'s inclusion is shocking largely for three reasons: limited reviews and word of mouth, being the fest's opening film (which has statistically been a curse for decades), and being the second TIFF premiere to place in a lineup where none were expected. The first, of course, being Peter Farrelly's *I Play Rocky* (you can read my in-depth thoughts on that [right here](/review.html?slug=i-play-rocky)).

*I Play Rocky* received outright mixed critical reviews, worse than *Being Heumann*, but in return, received thunderous acclaim from TIFF audiences, with near-unanimous praise for Anthony Ippolito among many other parts of the film from audiences. A somewhat fitting fate for *I Play Rocky*, coming in as the underdog, with an underdog story about a guy trying to make an underdog story.

On the flip side, *Prima Facie* and *Misty Green* are two of the most high-profile films at TIFF to completely flop. While *Misty Green* managed to secure good critic scores, *Prima Facie* did not, with an atrocious Rotten Tomatoes score of 31%. A fitting contrast may be that while *Prima Facie* ended up with fine audience scores, the same cannot be said for *Misty Green*, receiving widely negative attention (read fellow Fantasy Filmball writer Sebastian's thoughts on the film [right here](/review.html?slug=misty-green)). TIFF also produced one particular critical flop that you may have heard about, being Zack Snyder's *The Last Photograph*. Many things can be said about the film (as I did in [my review](/review.html?slug=the-last-photograph)), but there's something to be noted about receiving a 16% on Rotten Tomatoes and a 16 on Metacritic, producing perhaps the worst scores of the entire festival season, with audience metrics clearly not doing much better.

In the end, TIFF results seemed mostly black and white, with the singular exception of *Your Mother Your Mother Your Mother*. The film received the most acclaim out of basically any film premiering at the festival. In the end though, audiences weren't completely sold on the film, and it now likely being Amazon's third priority puts it in a very negative position if it wants a future this awards season.

<h2>NYFF</h2>

2026 brought one of the strongest sets of NYFF premieres in recent memory. Among the four major films to note, the worst performance may be that of *A Statement*, which didn't even receive necessarily bad reactions. The consensus towards *Spotlight* director Tom McCarthy's film is positive, just not particularly in a way where it will be seen as a major future contender this awards season.

*Godzilla Minus Zero* isn't receiving critical reactions too different from McCarthy's film, but given a lower bar for a victory, it absolutely passes what it needs. *Godzilla Minus Zero* doesn't seem to be as universally beloved as *Minus One* (and given a stronger visual effects field, is unlikely to recreate that film's Oscar win), but it still is liked by audiences and critics to a point where it could easily reach the bar it needs to secure a Visual Effects nomination. Perhaps the biggest motivator for this NYFF premiere is the premiere itself. A festival showing for a Godzilla movie may place the film at a good point to compete for awards this year, and while awards like Best Picture will likely be off the table, it will be interesting to see how it shakes up the Sound category.

It isn't fair to the two up-in-the-air films to place them as equal in their debut. The first is Pedro Pascal's *Behemoth*, receiving mixed-to-positive reactions. The film's awards life seems limited, with prospects pointing to Actor and Original Score at best, potentially with a song in the mix. *Behemoth* doesn't seem to be bad by any means, and it may hold some stake within this awards season, but it truly seems like it could stay or go.

The irony of *The Social Reckoning* flopping is the near-simultaneous debut of *Artificial*, which has been dubbed the unofficial true sequel to David Fincher's *The Social Network*. *Artificial* received reviews somewhat similar to *Behemoth*, if a little more positive, but it seems to be in a much better position to see awards. The film seems positioned to strike in categories such as Song, Score, Supporting Actor, Lead Actor, and Original Screenplay, all of which were quite acclaimed for the film. *Artificial* seems perfectly poised to contend, which makes the statistics around it all the more confusing. In the last two years, there have been major efforts to bring Guadagnino back into the awards race, but in the end, all of *Challengers*, *Queer*, and *After the Hunt* wrapped up with zero Oscar nominations. The New York Film Festival also has a negative addendum to it. Last year's *Marty Supreme* situation is unprecedented, and when neglecting that, the festival's last Best Picture nominee was Martin Scorsese's *The Irishman*. The question lingering for *Artificial* is simple: will its topical subject matter and particular acclaim push it above the stats it faces?

Similar to New York, London is rarely responsible for a huge portion of awards season debuts, but this year showed a notable slate. The big headline here is the overwhelming acclaim for Laika's *Wildwood*. In a somewhat competitive animated category, *Wildwood* soared to phenomenal critical and audience scores, now a largely consensus prediction to be nominated, if not win the category. *Wildwood* wasn't the only big animated debut at the festival though, with Brad Bird's *Ray Gunn* sharing the spotlight. That said, *Ray Gunn* wasn't quite as well received, with mixed-positive reviews leaving it somewhat in danger, but it still could prove strong with Netflix behind it.

*The Last Photograph* is the most overwhelmingly panned film at this year's fall festivals, and likely on some sort of podium for the decade. To even near such a level of negative reaction is a difficult endeavor, so it just so happens that doing so is Taika Waititi's biggest achievement in half a decade. *Klara and the Sun* received decent audience reactions but some of the year's worst critic reactions, making flops like *Digger* and *The Social Reckoning* look like juggernauts. While the expectations weren't particularly high, it's interesting to note that Taika Waititi may have had one of the most disappointing falls in filmmaking since his Oscar win for *Jojo Rabbit*.

To many, fall festivals signal the start of awards season, but their ending can be just as significant as their start. With the festivals over, what remains is a set of contenders, a roster of studios, and awards coming in hot. The time has arrived for the competitions to commence. May the best film win, and may *The Last Photograph* never be released.
