**“Are you following me?”**

The romcom is a subgenre that I’m concerned is in some serious danger. I’m not the only one to feel this way, and I’m sure it’s been on the mind of many a romantic for years now. Gone are the days when a meet-cute, adorable sidequests, a wrench in the works at the end of the second act, and a heartwarming declaration of love can get you hundreds of millions of dollars, as long as you found stars with the right chemistry and a director who knew how to let the film breathe. I’ve grown up comforted by the classics of yesteryear in the subgenre, revisiting favorites from the '90s and '00s time and time again, but looking dejectedly at the lack of new life. However, some of the few successful bouts I’ve noticed take a strange, high-concept approach and run with it to achieve new heights of both humor and heart. I think of *Palm Springs*, an existential crisis that served as a warm hug during the pandemic, or *Lisa Frankenstein*, which revamps the classical tale of Victor’s monster with zaniness, irreverence, and surprising depth. With these in mind, I walked into *One Night Only* excited to see how "romcom Purge" could wring something new out of a genre I’m convinced still has more to give.

Perhaps the greatest of the pleasant surprises on display here is that this is a genuinely good script. The premise really does come across as the type of film where the pitch was made and then no one knew how to construct a film around it, but this is a movie that actually feels interested in using its core concept to push the characters forward. This is a character-focused film, which is typically the struggle I’ve noticed with high-concept works of recent years, and essentially a requirement to make romance believable. A strong structure and surprisingly laugh-out-loud dialogue meant that I really did feel invested throughout, even when this hits all the beats you’d expect it to before the opening logos start.

A romcom is wholly and unequivocally reliant on its leads, and here I’d say they went one for two. Callum Turner is hovering just around the level of competence, but he seems unsure whether to play his character as a nice guy gone wild or a bad boy holding it together, and the tonal dissonance leads to some issues getting on board with his end of the storyline. I think he warmed my heart a bit by the end, but that may be more due to his excellent scene partner. Monica Barbaro turns in a surprisingly effortless and wonderfully fitting performance as Allison, giving the audience someone to latch onto immediately with her lovable awkwardness, hints of dork, and ineffable charm. She’s a character I liked at the start and loved at the end, and her romance with Turner feels believable primarily due to her work here. The imbalance of strength between the two leads makes this a bit of an uneven viewing, but Barbaro can handle the load well enough to make this quite a pleasant watch.

Unfortunately, there are many clear weaknesses holding this back from entering the annals of couch rewatches for future years, and I place blame primarily at the feet of director Will Gluck. It truly feels that he had no interest in doing anything with the written material beyond the absolute bare minimum, and his voice causes the drab visual aesthetic, nonstop needle drops, and occasionally odd pacing to stand at attention throughout the film. This is also a film that features many potentially interesting supporting characters that yearn for more screentime but aren’t given any. Both Maya Hawke and Julia Fox have some fun with their few minutes of screentime, but neither is afforded any real chance to make an impression. In general, this is a two-horse race with all others sidelined, making it difficult to walk out with a true lovable sidekick that I think helps bring some romcoms over the line from good to great.

Overall, this is a movie that does an alright job at what it sets out to do. It’s funny, it’s cute, and it plays the audience well. When I got to the classic, clichéd beats at the end, there was a little skip in my heart that I always look out for with a good romance movie. I can analyze and I can critique films as long as I want, but art is emotion at the end of the day. If two people sharing a tender moment on the silver screen makes me smile, that’s good enough for me, even if the road to get there was bumpy.

Also, if you have questions about the functionality of the premise, they will answer them. It’s a good-enough explanation where you stop thinking about it during the movie, but if you think about it a lot, it gets really funny, which the film seems fairly self-aware of.
