*No Other Land*, the winner of the 2025 Academy Award for Best Documentary Feature, followed Basel, a Palestinian activist who documented the systematic destruction of his home village by IDF soldiers. He met and eventually formed a bond with Israeli journalist Yuval Abraham, in a film that’s both personal and character-driven, as we witnessed the lead-up to the October 7th attacks through the eyes of these personalities. Now, Abraham and *No Other Land* co-director, Rachel Szor, have released a follow-up that is neither personal nor character-driven: an almost surgical analysis of the inhumane methods used by Israeli intelligence in their response to the attacks.

*NAZA* takes place almost entirely on the moonlit rooftops of Tel Aviv, where Abraham interviews two dozen Israeli officers and soldiers, silhouetted to hide their identities. They confess to widespread tactics used to target members of Hamas, with seemingly little regard for civilian life. “NAZA” refers to the term “Nezek Agavi”, meaning “collateral damage”. Through AI and other intelligence methods, every potential Hamas target is assigned a *NAZA* value to denote how many civilians are expected to be killed in said strikes.

The various interviews are interspersed with voyeuristic footage of Tel Aviv citizens through the windows of their homes and offices. Watching TV, exercising, washing the dishes. There are multiple ways of interpreting this stylistic choice. The juxtaposition of Israeli citizens going about their everyday lives with the suffering of Palestinian citizens in Gaza, less than a hundred kilometres away, is haunting, to say the least. Similarly, you can interpret the Israelis through the windows as the Palestinians themselves, in the minutes prior to having their lives taken by an airstrike designed to collapse the whole building, an act that is discussed at length by the Israeli whistleblowers.

While *No Other Land* utilised pathos to evoke a strong emotional reaction to the injustices on display, *NAZA* relies entirely on ethos and logos instead. We hear the very perpetrators analyse in detail the tactics and methodology behind the atrocities. It’s reminiscent of Jonathan Glazer's Academy Award winning, *The Zone of Interest*, in that it maintains the audience at a cold distance, as we’re stricken by the stark contrast between the horror of what’s going on and the banality with which it’s being described (one officer goes on to admit that the attacks are often treated by him and his co-workers as a video game). It was no surprise, then, to find Glazer, the director of *The Zone of Interest,* and his collaborator James Wilson credited as producers of *NAZA*.

Like Glazer’s *The Zone of Interest* and *Under the Skin* before it, there’s something deeply meditative about the way *NAZA* broaches its subject, framing these interviews through shots of the Tel Aviv windows. Which actually leads me to my only real criticism of the film: the window shots are visually far more interesting and thematically rich than the silhouetted footage of the interviews themselves, yet most of the runtime shows the latter as opposed to the former. The sequences that consist of only the interviews ended up feeling a bit tedious; once we’ve seen one silhouetted face, we’ve seen them all. Had Abraham and Szor opted instead to overlay the audio of the interviews with the voyeuristic footage, with the occasional shot of the interviews themselves, I feel the result would have felt more like a cohesive work of art. Make the windows the film, not merely a recurring motif. Even the subtitle on the film’s poster — “A WINDOW INTO SYSTEMS OF MASS KILLING” — seems to recognise the windows as *NAZA*’s most interesting feature; I just wish they had leaned even further into it in the film itself.

That qualm aside, I found *NAZA* to be a step up from its Academy Award-winning predecessor, though I have no doubts that the average voter will disagree. As engaging as *No Other Land* is, I always felt like it succeeded more on a journalistic level than on a formal or artistic level. Of course, when it comes to what either film is seeking to accomplish, any emotional or artistic goal is surely secondary to revealing the atrocities of the Israel-Palestine conflict to audiences. Any criticism I can possibly offer comes from a place of privilege, as an admirer of art and the cinematic medium, such that writing a review of *NAZA* seems almost insensitive. Both *NAZA* and *No Other Land* are essential documents of a human rights crisis of which we must all be more aware. Unlike *No Other Land,* which struggled to find widespread distribution, *NAZA* is in theatres across the US and will reportedly be available to stream later this month. I urge anyone reading this to seek it out.
